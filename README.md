038862 Recommendation

1. Overall Assessment
This manuscript introduces MADS (Multi-Agent Dynamic System), a clinician-facing framework that converts multimodal EHR data into patient-specific decision trees for cardiovascular medication recommendation. The central claim is that grounding LLM outputs in an executable, evidence-traceable decision structure improves auditability, reduces drug-drug interaction errors, and yields higher medication-label concordance under physician oversight. The design priority — interpretable execution over unconstrained generation — is conceptually coherent.
The ablation result that removing the Decision component causes larger Micro-F1 degradation than removing either RAG or agent Collaboration is the paper's most credible finding: it directly tests the mechanistic claim. However, the entire evidence base rests on a retrospective single-center cohort from one Chinese tertiary hospital, evaluation is conducted exclusively at medication-category level rather than full prescribing specificity, and the most clinically significant claim — that AI-assisted physicians outperform standalone AI — is supported by a 17-patient matched analysis without confidence intervals or inter-rater reliability. These are structural rather than revisable problems.

2. Strengths
The ablation design is the paper's strongest contribution. By isolating w/o Decision, w/o RAG, and w/o Collaboration, the authors directly test their mechanistic claim rather than simply reporting headline performance. The ranking — Decision removal causing the largest Micro-F1 drop, followed by RAG, followed by Collaboration — is internally consistent with the thesis that structured tree execution outperforms agent multiplicity as a source of performance gain.
Encoding uncertainty as an explicit yes/no/uncertain branch, rather than suppressing it into a binary output, is architecturally principled. The robustness finding that natural missingness causes less degradation than simulated evidence dropout — because the multi-agent workflow recovers partial signal from redundant chart context — is a clinically plausible and non-obvious result that strengthens the uncertainty-handling argument.
The safety decomposition separating overt failures (drug-drug interactions, high-risk false positives) from contextual safety failures (contraindication, renal/bleeding risk) is analytically mature. Reducing major drug-drug interaction errors from 1.48% to 0.15% is meaningful. The authors appropriately note that contraindication-related errors worsened from 24.24% to 26.73%, resisting the temptation to report safety selectively.

3. Weaknesses
External validity is unaddressed. All 3,015 cases originate from a single tertiary hospital in Chengdu. The manuscript never reports demographic characteristics — age distribution, sex, comorbidity burden — of the cohort. For a cardiovascular polypharmacy system, this omission prevents any assessment of generalizability and renders the safety findings institution-specific. A secondary external cohort, even smaller, was not attempted.
The evaluation target is a structural concession inadequately defended. All benchmarking is conducted at medication-category level, not at full prescribing resolution (dose, timing, route, monitoring). Comparing Micro-F1 against gemini-3-flash-preview conflates a classification task with a clinical decision task. The manuscript does not demonstrate that category-level accuracy predicts full prescribing accuracy in any subset.
The clinician evaluation is underpowered. The matched concordance comparison covers 17 patient cases and 75 physician ratings from six clinicians; no sample size calculation or inter-rater reliability statistic is reported. The finding that AI-assisted physicians achieved Micro-F1 of 63% versus 57% for standalone AI is presented without confidence intervals, making it statistically uninterpretable.
The ground truth construction is inadequately described. How conflicting labels across medication orders, discharge prescriptions, and narrative reports were resolved is not stated. The medication-category taxonomy is referenced but not provided in the main text, making all F1 values non-reproducible.

4. Editorial Decision
Reject. The decision-tree execution framework is conceptually differentiated and the ablation is well-designed, but the single-center design without external validation, absent demographic reporting, category-level-only evaluation, and a 17-patient clinician study without confidence intervals collectively preclude credible translational inference. These deficiencies require new data collection, not revision. The work is better scoped for npj Digital Medicine or JAMIA, contingent on adequate demographic characterization and ground truth transparency.

5. Suggested Reviewer Expertise
Reviewers should cover: (1) multi-agent LLM system design and RAG architectures for structured clinical decision tasks; (2) multilabel classification evaluation with specific familiarity with Micro-F1, Macro-F1, and Jaccard overlap as applied to EHR-derived label sets; (3) clinical decision support evaluation methodology, including inter-rater reliability, concordance study design, and power analysis for human-AI comparison trials; (4) cardiovascular pharmacology and polypharmacy management in multimorbid patients with direct tertiary prescribing experience; (5) health AI regulatory science, particularly SaMD classification and the distinction between decision support and autonomous prescribing.

6. State-of-the-Art Literature Review
The problem space MADS occupies — multi-agent LLM collaboration for EHR-grounded clinical decision support — has generated a dense cluster of directly competing work in 2024–2025 that the manuscript incompletely engages. The six most directly relevant papers are as follows.
MDAgents (Kim et al., NeurIPS 2024) is the closest architectural antecedent. MDAgents introduces a framework that adapts the collaboration structure of LLMs dynamically based on medical task complexity, achieving best performance in seven of ten medical benchmarks. MADS's specialist agent consultation triggered by evidence gaps is a domain-constrained variant of this complexity-adaptive collaboration mechanism; the manuscript cites it as a baseline but does not articulate the architectural distinction. OpenReview
ColaCare (Zhu et al., WWW 2025) targets the same EHR multi-agent integration problem. ColaCare integrates domain-specific expert models with LLMs through a Multidisciplinary Team-inspired DoctorAgent/MetaAgent architecture, incorporating RAG-based medical guideline retrieval, and demonstrates superior performance on clinical mortality and readmission prediction across three EHR datasets. ColaCare and MADS share both the MDT-simulation motivation and the RAG module design; MADS does not differentiate against ColaCare's evidence-grounding approach. ACM Digital Library
MedAgentBoard (Zhu et al., NeurIPS 2025) is the most damaging concurrent finding. MedAgentBoard demonstrates that multi-agent collaboration methods do not consistently outperform the best-performing single LLMs and remain substantially outperformed by conventional methods on EHR predictive modeling tasks, with performance differences attributable to the underlying base LLM rather than the multi-agent strategy itself. This directly challenges MADS's framing of multi-agent design as a performance driver, and the manuscript's own ablation result — that Collaboration removal causes the smallest degradation — is consistent with this finding. The authors should have engaged this explicitly. OpenReview
Multi-LLM Collaboration for Medication Recommendation (Sanchez & Hitaj, 2024) addresses medication recommendation from clinical notes using chemistry-inspired LLM ensemble compatibility modeling, evaluated on patient-specific recommendation accuracy and collaboration stability. It shares MADS's medication recommendation scope and multi-model collaboration premise and constitutes a direct methodological comparator the manuscript omits entirely.
CPGPrompt (2025) converts clinical practice guidelines into structured LLM-executable decision trees integrated with an auditable chatbot interface. CPGPrompt translates clinical guidelines into structured, LLM-executable decision trees to produce transparent, auditable clinical referral decisions — a design principle nearly identical to MADS's decision scaffold, predating this submission and not cited. arXiv
LLM as Clinical Decision Support for Medication Safety (Cell Reports Medicine, 2025) provides the most direct safety-auditing comparator. This prospective cross-over study evaluated five RAG-enhanced LLMs across 91 prescribing error scenarios in 16 clinical specialties as alternatives to rule-based pharmacovigilance alert systems. MADS's safety claims — particularly the drug-drug interaction error reduction — should be situated against this parallel RAG-safety paradigm operating at comparable clinical specificity. Cell Press

039342 Disentanglement"

1. Overall Assessment
This manuscript proposes RaSD, a framework for pre-training medical image foundation models exclusively on rule-based synthetic data generated from Gaussian distributions, eliminating reliance on real patient images. The central claim is that synthetic-only pre-training matches or surpasses real-data pre-training across six imaging modalities, 48 datasets, and 56 downstream tasks. If reproducibly substantiated, this would represent a meaningful shift in how MIFMs are developed.
The primary concern is a systematically tilted comparison framework. RaSD is benchmarked against real-data pre-trained models using RaSD's own fine-tuning protocol, and the paper lacks any ablation isolating the prototype disentangling learning (PDL) objective from plain synthetic-data pre-training. The second concern is the complete absence of clinical validation or demographic subgroup analysis.
2. Strengths
The scale of empirical evaluation is a genuine strength. Benchmarking across 56 tasks with statistical significance testing against scratch baseline and best comparator is unusual and elevates the evidentiary standard above typical ablation-only papers in this space.
The online streaming synthesis paradigm addresses a real infrastructure bottleneck. Table 1 demonstrates a 13% wall-clock reduction and complete elimination of storage overhead at 1,000-sample scale, which is practically consequential.
The cross-modal generalization results are noteworthy. RaSD-2D, pre-trained without mammography data, performs within 5% of Mammo-CLIP across 80% of mammography comparisons — a result that directly and non-trivially supports the core theoretical claim.
3. Weaknesses
The PDL ablation is absent. Without toggling the prototype separation and clustering losses while holding the synthesis engine constant, gains cannot be attributed to disentanglement versus synthetic data volume. The Anatomix comparison is insufficient because it differs along multiple axes simultaneously.
The Gaussian assumption's failure regime is underreported. Tasks where RaSD underperforms — ATLAS-MR (stroke lesions), several mammography tasks — map precisely to domains exceeding Gaussian expressivity. The authors should quantify what anatomical or pathological complexity features predict failure.
There is no demographic subgroup analysis across 56 tasks spanning chest X-ray, mammography, retinal, and pathology modalities — all domains with documented disparities. This omission is disqualifying at this tier and cannot be deferred.
4. Editorial Decision
Reject. The missing PDL ablation is the experiment required to support the paper's primary mechanistic claim. The complete absence of fairness analysis across sensitive clinical modalities is disqualifying. If revised and resubmitted, reviewers should adjudicate: (1) whether PDL contribution can be isolated from data volume effects; (2) whether cross-modal results hold across demographic strata; and (3) whether performance gaps versus domain-specific models are clinically meaningful.

5. Suggested Reviewer Expertise
Reviewers should include expertise in: (1) large-scale self-supervised and synthetic pre-training for 3D medical image segmentation, specifically contrastive and prototype-based representation learning; (2) generative models and rule-based synthesis pipelines for medical imaging, including evaluation of distributional realism; (3) domain generalization theory and cross-modality transfer in vision models; (4) algorithmic fairness and demographic bias assessment in medical AI, particularly for imaging-based classification and detection; and (5) clinical radiology or diagnostic pathology, with experience evaluating AI-assisted workflows in real deployment contexts.

6. State-of-the-Art Literature Review
The past three years have seen the medical image foundation model field bifurcate into competing paradigms. Real-data supervised pre-training at scale is represented by SuPreM (Li et al., ICLR 2024 oral) on AbdomenAtlas with 9,262 annotated CT volumes, and VoCo (Wu et al., TPAMI 2025) extending to 160K CT volumes across 50+ downstream tasks. Generative augmentation paradigms have also matured: Wang et al.'s MINIM (Nature Medicine 2025) demonstrated self-improving generative synthesis for multi-organ, multi-modality pre-training; Sheng et al. (Nature Biomedical Engineering 2025) showed synthetic data boosts real-data foundation models when used as augmentation rather than replacement. The RaSD framework occupies a third position — synthetic-only, rule-based, zero real data — which Anatomix partially explored but with narrower modality scope and an appearance-suppression design that actively degrades intensity-stable modalities. A concurrent preprint, "Fake It Right" (2025), addresses similar territory with anatomy-informed spatial point processes and reports Dice improvements of 1.74% over FDSL baselines on BTCV and MSD. RaSD's contribution is genuine but narrower than claimed: it establishes Gaussian synthesis is competitive on normal-anatomy segmentation and delivers real infrastructure advantages, but does not establish superiority on pathological tasks and does not engage with MINIM or vesselFM as directly competing paradigms.
Closely related papers sharing similar scope (past 3 years):
Anatomix — Hirano et al. (2024, arXiv). Rule-based randomized synthesis for 3D MIFM pre-training using random intensity distributions; the most direct predecessor. Differs from RaSD in focusing on appearance-unstable modalities and lacking a disentanglement objective. RaSD consistently outperforms it on CT tasks, and the authors' own ablations implicitly argue that appearance suppression is the limiting design choice.
SuPreM — Li et al. (ICLR 2024, oral). Supervised pre-training on AbdomenAtlas-8 (9,262 CT volumes, 25 annotated organ classes) using Swin UNETR. Establishes the real-data ceiling RaSD is compared against on CT segmentation. A direct and strong baseline; the margin by which RaSD beats SuPreM on TotalSegmentator and IRCADb warrants deeper scrutiny.
VoCo / Large-Scale-Medical — Wu et al. (CVPR 2024; TPAMI 2025). Volume contrastive pre-training leveraging geometric context priors across 160K CT volumes and 50+ tasks. Represents the SSL real-data paradigm at maximum scale; RaSD's competitive performance against VoCo (160K) despite using zero real data is the most striking empirical claim in the manuscript.
MINIM — Wang et al. (Nature Medicine 2025). Self-improving generative foundation model using text-conditioned image synthesis for multi-organ, multi-modality generation. Directly challenges RaSD's design choice of rule-based over learned generative synthesis; the authors do not engage with this work.
Synthetic Data Boosts Medical Foundation Models — Sheng et al. (Nature Biomedical Engineering 2025). Demonstrates that synthetic augmentation of real training corpora yields systematic gains. Directly contradicts RaSD's "synthetic-only is sufficient" framing by showing hybrid approaches outperform either alone in head-to-head comparisons. This paper should have been discussed.
"Fake It Right" — (arXiv 2025). Anatomy-informed synthetic supervised pre-training using constrained spatial point processes to encode topological organ relationships. Reports 1.74% Dice improvement over FDSL baselines on BTCV and MSD, directly overlapping RaSD's evaluation datasets. RaSD does not reference or compare against this concurrent work.

039409 1. Overall Assessment
This manuscript presents LUMEN, a multimodal deep learning framework integrating chest CT image features with LLM-generated chain-of-thought reasoning summaries (KCoTS) for automated prediction of three pulmonary dysfunction phenotypes — FEV₁/FVC <70%, FEV₁% <80%, and TLC% <80% — across a multicenter cohort of 5,401 patients. The framework combines CheXNet-based image feature extraction, MedBert text embedding, bidirectional cross-attention fusion, and a three-stage confidence-aware training strategy (CATS) anchored to the reliability of DeepSeek-V3.2-Exp outputs.
The central question — whether LLM-structured clinical reasoning can meaningfully improve over raw CT text in a multimodal pulmonary classifier — is legitimate. However, the image backbone is CheXNet, a DenseNet121 pretrained on chest radiographs, not CT, and 96.1% of the derivation cohort originates from a single center. These two concerns are fundamental and govern the editorial decision.

2. Strengths
The CATS training strategy is the manuscript's most distinctive contribution. The three-stage curriculum — prioritizing high-confidence LLM outputs, then low-confidence image-driven learning, then joint fine-tuning — addresses a genuine problem in hybrid LLM–DL pipelines: unreliable surrogate labels from the auxiliary reasoning module. The loss formulation (Equations 4.1–4.4) and bootstrap stability analysis (100 resampling iterations, Mann-Whitney U across stages) provide adequate mathematical and empirical support.
The inclusion of restrictive ventilatory impairment prediction (TLC% <80%) is clinically meaningful. TLC requires body plethysmography in practice; CT-based prediction is genuinely impactful if robustly validated.
The modality ablation is rigorous. Five competing models trained on identical datasets cleanly demonstrate that LLM-mediated text restructuring — not mere text inclusion — drives the LUMEN gains, since CT+CTR underperforms CT alone while CT+KCoTS surpasses it.

3. Weaknesses
CheXNet was pretrained on 2D chest radiographs from ChestX-ray14. Applying it to uniformly sampled 256×256 coronal CT slices is methodologically unsupported. No ablation compares this against a CT-native backbone. The entire image representation is therefore unvalidated relative to what a competent CT-pretrained alternative would yield.
Of 5,002 derivation patients, 4,807 originate from one unnamed center. The two external validation sets contain 206 and 193 patients, yielding wide confidence intervals (e.g., Task 3, Validation B: AUC 0.768, 95% CI 0.663–0.858). No subgroup performance by center, sex, age, or smoking status is reported, which is unacceptable for a paper advancing a deployed clinical tool.
The framework depends on DeepSeek-V3.2-Exp at inference time, yet no empirical performance data are provided for the CT-only fallback condition — the real-world operating mode when the LLM is unavailable or returns low-confidence output. No calibration metrics are reported alongside AUC.

4. Editorial Decision
Reject. The CATS training strategy and modality ablation design are credible contributions, but two fundamental deficiencies prevent publication. The radiograph-pretrained CheXNet backbone is unevaluated against CT-native alternatives, and the derivation cohort is overwhelmingly single-center despite multicenter framing. A revised submission requires: (1) ablation against a CT-pretrained image encoder; (2) center-stratified and sex/smoking-stratified performance; (3) calibration reporting; and (4) empirical evaluation of the CT-only fallback mode.

5. Suggested Reviewer Expertise
The manuscript requires reviewers with expertise in: (1) 3D and 2D CNN/ViT architectures for chest CT analysis, including transfer learning from radiograph versus CT pretraining regimes; (2) LLM integration in clinical NLP pipelines, specifically chain-of-thought prompting and confidence-aware training in hybrid DL systems; (3) multimodal feature fusion architectures, including cross-attention mechanisms for image-text alignment; (4) pulmonary function physiology and clinical interpretation of spirometric and plethysmographic thresholds in obstructive and restrictive disease; and (5) clinical AI evaluation methodology, with attention to external validation design, calibration, and subgroup fairness in imbalanced classification tasks.

6. State-of-the-Art Literature Review (Past 3 Years)
The CT-based pulmonary function prediction space has expanded substantially since 2022. Park et al. (2023) demonstrated that dilated 3D CNNs on low-dose screening CT predict FEV₁/FVC<70% with AUC ~0.90 but sensitivity below 65%, motivating class-imbalance correction — a gap LUMEN directly addresses. Lin et al. (2025) proposed AutoCOPD, integrating CT images, reports, and questionnaires via XGBoost with binary keyword features; text inclusion reduced validation AUC from 0.860 to 0.859, establishing the methodological foil LUMEN attempts to surpass. Deng et al. (2025, APL Bioengineering) proposed MMDF-Net integrating CT images, spirometric indices, and environmental data via dual-tower cross-modal contrastive learning for COPD stratification — a structurally similar but unengaged competitor. A parallel BMC Pulmonary Medicine study (2025) used MLP fusion of CT-derived and clinical features to estimate continuous PFT parameters in 2,408 COPD patients. LUMEN does not engage with the contrastive learning multimodal literature, the quantitative CT tradition (emphysema scoring, air trapping from COPDGene), or BioViL-T/MedCLIP-style vision-language pretraining, all of which represent natural competing baselines.
Further Literature — Closely Scoped Papers (Past 3 Years)

Lin et al. (2025) — AutoCOPD: Multicenter COPD prediction integrating CT images, free-text reports (binary keyword encoding), and patient questionnaires via XGBoost. Directly comparable multimodal scope; establishes the failure of naive text integration and is LUMEN's most proximate predecessor.
Park et al. (2023) — Dilated 3D CNN on low-dose chest CT for FEV₁/FVC<70% and FEV₁%<80% prediction in health screening participants. Unimodal imaging baseline; cited by LUMEN but not ablated against with an equivalent CT backbone.
Deng et al. (2025, APL Bioengineering) — MMDF-Net for COPD risk stratification fusing chest CT, spirometric indicators, and environmental exposure via dual-tower cross-modal contrastive learning. Most structurally similar contemporary competitor; unacknowledged by the manuscript.
Ueda et al. (2024) — ConvNet applied to chest X-ray for pulmonary function prediction in a Japanese cohort; reported accuracy comparable to CT-based approaches. Provides a cross-modality comparison point and highlights the unresolved question of whether CT provides meaningful advantage over radiograph for PFT surrogacy.
Jeong et al. (2024) — CT-derived quantitative features (emphysema index, air trapping) combined with clinical variables for FEV₁/FVC classification via ensemble learning. Represents the interpretable quantitative CT tradition that LUMEN's black-box CheXNet backbone bypasses without justification.
JMIR (2025) — F-Sum+CoT+RAG framework: LLM-based pulmonary disease prediction from radiology reports using feature summarization, chain-of-thought prompting, and hybrid retrieval-augmented generation across four disease categories (tuberculosis, lung cancer, pneumonia, healthy). Directly parallel LLM prompting strategy for respiratory diagnosis from text; LUMEN's authors should have engaged with this work to situate their CT-PFT prompt design.

Authors: Luo, Shu, Zhou, Reyes

1. Overall Assessment
This manuscript investigates whether clinically guided attention supervision — implemented as a cross-attention Dice loss (Attention Aligner) within a ViT-B/Med-KEBERT vision-language model — can simultaneously improve fairness, robustness, OOD stability, calibration, and few-shot resilience in chest X-ray classification. The framing around "clinical readiness" rather than diagnostic accuracy is timely, and the evaluation design is more systematic than most prior explanation-guided learning (EGL) work. However, the scope of the central claim exceeds what the evidence supports. The paper does not establish transfer beyond chest radiography, beyond the ViT-B/Med-KEBERT architecture, or beyond bounding-box supervision. The incremental alignment experiment manipulates annotation retention probability via Bernoulli dropout, not supervision strength in any mechanistically grounded sense, leaving the non-monotonic optimum underspecified causally. These are the two concerns most likely to determine the editorial decision.

2. Strengths
The training corpus — 667,616 images from seven publicly available datasets spanning multiple continents, acquisition protocols, and patient demographics — substantially reduces the risk that gains reflect a single-site idiosyncrasy, and is more heterogeneous than most prior EGL studies, which have typically operated on single-source CheXpert or NIH ChestX-ray14 splits.
The randomized-attention ablation is methodologically strong. Replacing expert-guided maps with randomly positioned geometric primitives per training call directly addresses whether gains arise from semantic alignment or generic attention regularization — a competing explanation that has historically undermined EGL claims. The finding that random attention degrades both fairness and calibration strengthens the causal inference considerably.
The multi-axis evaluation framework assesses 60 corruption conditions, coefficient-of-variation across four external cohorts, ECE over three independent runs, and few-shot fine-tuning from N=0 to N=10,000 within a single study — more comprehensive than existing EGL benchmarks.
The practical framing of resilience results is effective. Translating AUC differences at N=100 into estimated annotation catch-up time (2.8 years for pneumothorax, 83 missed true-positive detections) using NIH ChestX-ray14 prevalence and a reported hospital CXR volume converts an abstract metric into a deployable, operationally interpretable quantity.

3. Weaknesses
The simultaneous improvement claim is overstated. Edema calibration shows no benefit (ECE: 19.4% vs. 19.4%), nodule/mass and pneumonia OOD variability show no reduction, and effusion favors maximum rather than intermediate alignment under sex-stratified evaluation. These exceptions are noted without mechanistic analysis. A paper asserting a unified training-time lever must explain when and why alignment fails, not merely attribute deviations to task-dependence.
The fairness analysis covers only sex and age, and only on CheXpert and JSRT — the two external datasets where demographic metadata happened to be available. Race and ethnicity, the attributes most prominently implicated in chest X-ray bias literature (Gichoya et al., The Lancet Digital Health, 2022; Seyyed-Kalantari et al., Nature Medicine, 2021), are absent entirely. Given that bounding-box annotations derive primarily from US academic hospital datasets (MIMIC-CXR, NIH ChestX-ray14), it is plausible that alignment encodes racially correlated anatomical priors — this is untested.
The incremental alignment experiment manipulates Bernoulli retention probability, conflating annotation availability with supervision intensity. The operationally meaningful quantity — what annotation coverage fraction is required to achieve the optimum — is never characterized. An experiment varying the proportion of annotated images directly is absent, and this substantially undermines the data-efficiency argument in Section 2.5.
Statistical reporting is inconsistent. No confidence intervals or significance tests appear for the OOD coefficient-of-variation comparisons in Figure 4b — the primary evidence for the stability claim. ECE is reported as point estimates without run-level variance. Given ECE's documented sensitivity to small test set size and that CheXpert (234 images) and JSRT (247 images) are small external benchmarks, these calibration improvements may not be statistically reliable.

4. Editorial Decision
Send for revision with conditions before peer review. The evaluation scope and randomized-attention ablation represent a genuine advance over prior EGL work. However, three issues must be resolved: (1) race/ethnicity-stratified fairness analysis or an explicit bound on why the evaluation datasets preclude it; (2) an annotation-coverage experiment replacing the retention-probability design; (3) bootstrap confidence intervals for OOD stability and calibration results. Reviewers should adjudicate whether attention map localization evidence is sufficient without direct probing of representations for demographic attribute encoding, and whether the non-monotonic optimum is robust to alternative operationalizations of alignment strength.

5. Suggested Reviewer Expertise
Reviewers should include specialists in (1) explanation-guided and attention supervision methods for vision transformers in medical imaging; (2) algorithmic fairness in chest radiograph classification, with specific experience in subgroup AUC gap analysis and OOD fairness trade-offs; (3) distribution shift and corruption robustness evaluation in clinical imaging AI; (4) probabilistic calibration of deep neural networks under small-sample regimes; and (5) a clinical radiologist with operational experience in multi-site CXR deployment capable of assessing whether the annotation burden reduction estimates are ecologically valid.

6. State-of-the-Art Literature Review (Past 3 Years)
The field has moved rapidly across three interacting fronts. On fairness limits, Yang et al. (Nature Medicine, 2024) demonstrated that standard fairness corrections in chest X-ray classifiers introduce a systematic OOD generalization penalty — fairer in-distribution models produced worse OOD AUC, larger ECE gaps, and reduced F1 across multiple modalities. This is the strongest existing counterargument to this manuscript's central thesis, and engagement with it remains insufficient: the authors cite it once in the discussion but do not test whether their aligned model violates this trade-off under equivalent evaluation conditions. Ktena et al. (Nature Medicine, 2024) showed that diffusion model-based demographic augmentation improves fairness in-distribution and OOD across histopathology, chest radiology, and dermatology — achieving up to 44.6% lower fairness gap in CXR — without supervised spatial annotation, directly competing with the Attention Aligner paradigm on the annotation-cost axis. On foundation models, Cai et al. (RayDINO, arXiv, 2024) trained a 307M-parameter DINOv2 vision transformer on 873k chest X-rays using pure self-supervision and evaluated across 11 datasets from four continents, reporting state-of-the-art results on 21 benchmarks alongside explicit age and sex bias analysis — a design that shares the multi-site, multi-axis framing of this manuscript but requires zero annotation overhead. On EGL itself, the H-EGL framework (Mortanges et al., MICCAI-adjacent, 2024) combines human-guided and self-supervised attention constraints, reducing annotation dependency while matching or exceeding fully supervised EGL on accuracy and robustness — a direct architectural alternative the authors should compare against rather than simply cite as future work.

039747 1. Overall Assessment
This manuscript evaluates image-to-image (I2I) diffusion as a privacy-preserving transformation for medical images, via LoRA fine-tuning of Stable Diffusion 1.4 on retinal fundus photographs (HPP, N=10,010 patients) and chest radiographs (NIH CXR-8, N=32,717). The central claim is that I2I conditioning preserves subtle physiological signals — including hemoglobin and creatinine — not specified in the text prompt, while offering a tunable privacy-utility tradeoff through noise strength modulation. Two concerns dominate. First, the privacy analysis is methodologically thin: nearest-neighbor retrieval under SSIM and DINOv2 represents a weak attack model, and CXR images remain highly re-identifiable at all noise strengths. Second, the downstream estimator architecture — BiT-ResNet v2 101 — is outdated relative to retinal foundation models such as RETFound and FLAIR, making the biomarker preservation advantage uncertain.
2. Strengths
The evaluation of clinical signal preservation extends beyond binary disease labels to continuous physiological phenotypes withheld from the conditioning prompt — hemoglobin, creatinine, waist circumference — making any preservation evidence of implicit image encoding rather than prompt reproduction. The cross-site transfer to UK Biobank (N=11,030) provides a genuine domain-shift benchmark. The continuous privacy-utility tradeoff curve (Figure 5), sweeping noise strength from 0.1 to 1.0 across seven phenotypes simultaneously, is operationally actionable in a way binary de-identification decisions are not. Data release on HuggingFace alongside LoRA weights and training code meets reproducibility standards most clinical AI papers fail.
3. Weaknesses
The privacy evaluation covers only SSIM and DINOv2 nearest-neighbor retrieval. No membership inference, linkage attack, or model inversion analysis is performed. CXR images remain re-identifiable at 63% top-1 SSIM accuracy at strength 0.9, a failure the paper buries in Discussion rather than treating as a central null result. The BiT-ResNet v2 101 estimator is architecturally obsolete; the biomarker preservation advantage of I2I over T2I could narrow substantially under RETFound-based encoders. Mixing experiments show no consistent benefit over standard augmentation when real data are available, but augmentation intensity is not ablated systematically. No subgroup analyses by sex, age, or ancestry are reported despite substantial demographic differences between HPP and UK Biobank.
4. Editorial Decision

1. Overall Assessment
CausalDP proposes a sequential VAE that disentangles four latent factors — positional (c), background (z), AMD-causative (s), and AMD-unrelated (v) — from longitudinal color fundus photographs to model AMD progression. The core claim is that explicit causal disentanglement prevents models from degenerating into generic anomaly detectors when co-existing ocular comorbidities introduce spurious correlations. The work is technically principled, grounded in Pearl's structural causal model framework, and supported by a theoretically motivated identifiability proof. The contamination robustness evaluation — injecting FFA-IR, DEN, and DDR samples at 0–20% mixing rates — is the paper's most compelling empirical contribution.
The primary concern is external validity. The entire evaluation rests on AREDS, a single NIH trial cohort collected under controlled imaging conditions from a predominantly white, US-based population. No independent real-world validation is presented. The subphenotyping analysis (RQ4), which carries the bulk of the clinical novelty claim, lacks outcome linkage, survival analysis, and ophthalmologist validation.

2. Strengths
The identifiability proof in the supplementary material meaningfully extends Khemakhem et al.'s nonlinear ICA results to a temporally structured, supervised setting, providing theoretical guarantees largely absent from competing methods including CHMM. The supervised signal from both disease labels y and complication labels A is a principled design choice that directly addresses the theoretical impossibility of unsupervised disentanglement established by Locatello et al. (2019).
The mixed-disease robustness benchmark is well-designed and directly validates the core motivation. Baseline models (DeepSeeNet, CAT-LSTM, LST, CHMM) all degrade substantially at 5–20% mixing rates; CausalDP retains 83.10% at 20% MR versus 81.20% for the nearest competitor. This is a reproducible and clinically meaningful stress test.
The temporal multi-view consistency objective — using bilateral fundus image pairs (left/right eye from the same visit) to supervise disentanglement of c from z, s, and v via Equations 4–7 — is technically elegant and avoids synthetic augmentation. The ablation study across four model variants (CausalDP^s^, CausalDP^t^, CausalDP^c^, full) provides clear attributional evidence for each component across Tables 3 and 4.

3. Weaknesses
The study is validated exclusively on AREDS. AREDS is a controlled clinical trial with standardized imaging protocols, not a real-world fundus repository. Claims of generalizability across "diverse datasets" are unsupported without validation on an independent cohort such as UK Biobank or a multi-ethnic clinical EHR-linked registry. This is not a revision-level gap — it represents a fundamental missing experiment.
The subphenotyping analysis (RQ4) is the paper's most clinically ambitious claim and its weakest. Three subtypes are derived from k-means clustering on averaged s_t features, with cluster selection justified only by DBI and CHI metrics. No cluster stability analysis, no survival curves, no hazard ratios, and no comparison against the AREDS 9-step grading system or established AMD phenotypes (e.g., reticular pseudodrusen) are provided. Ophthalmologist adjudication of whether the three subtypes correspond to clinically recognized variants is entirely absent.

Decision Recommendation
Reject, not major revision. Here is the reasoning:
The three reviewers collectively identify problems that cannot be addressed through revision alone without substantially rebuilding the paper. Specifically: the interpretability framework is structurally weak (not just underreported), the statistical evaluation design has leakage and baseline selection problems that require re-running experiments, and the pharmacological conflation of indication/contraindication as equivalent token types is a conceptual flaw, not a presentation gap.

Summary of Reviewer Concerns
Reviewer 1 (Gwydion Williams) — Thorough, Mixed-to-Critical
Five major concerns: (1) the claimed efficiency advantage is never actually measured — there is no analysis of how many tests DxEvolve requests per case, whether this number decreases with experience, or whether confident diagnoses are reached with fewer interventions over time; this is the central practical claim and it is unvalidated; (2) no frontier model baselines (Claude, GPT-4o, Gemini) are tested, which is a critical omission since the gains may reflect the DCR framework rather than DxEvolve specifically — or conversely, that frontier models already perform comparably without it; (3) the early/late DCP discussion obscures a two-regime picture: early DCPs drive most gains, late DCPs are marginal refinements, yet the paper frames them as uniformly progressive; (4) using the DCP repository to standardize the Chinese PLA cohort translations introduces circularity — the same corpus shaping the test data is the one being tested; (5) the cross-disease generalizability claim rests primarily on UTI and liver abscess, both of which are abdominal and the sample sizes are very small (UTI n=11, appendicitis n=30).
Nine minor comments cover figure clarity, ground truth labeling, interpretability of the "incorrect experience rate" metric, and the framing of clinician comparison as "surpassing" rather than "comparable."
Reviewer 3 (Antonis Armoundas) — Most Comprehensive and Damaging
Ten major concerns, organized around three core problems:
The "self-evolution" framing is misleading. The DCP construction explicitly uses ground-truth diagnosis labels, correctness flags, rule-based feedback, and clinician orders — this is supervised, label-aware case-memory consolidation, not autonomous clinical learning. The authors need to either reframe the mechanism or run ablations distinguishing raw case retrieval from DCP retrieval, label-removed DCPs, test-ordering-only guidance, and nearest-neighbor retrieval.
Several workflow-alignment results are partly prompt-enforced. The agent is instructed in its system prompt to perform physical examination, labs, imaging, experience search, and guideline search before diagnosis — this directly inflates workflow-consistency and guideline-adherence metrics. An ablation without these prompt constraints is necessary.
The external validation is too small and insufficiently reported. N=293 overall, with UTI (n=11) being the basis for several generalizability claims. The paper requires exact case counts, confidence intervals per disease and language stratum, and sensitivity analyses excluding the smallest samples. The claim of 17.1% gain on "uncovered categories" is based on only two disease categories.
Additional concerns: possible evidence leakage in the cholecystitis example trace (future imaging findings appear in the experience search before being acquired); the human comparison should not be framed as "surpassing"; governance claims are conceptual and undemonstrated; statistical reporting uses p-values without uncertainty quantification across backbone models or seeds.
Handling Editor (Eric Wang)
Rates this as borderline OTR/Reject. The framework is conceptually fresh and the process-level analysis adds substance, but the dataset is small (under 3K encounters), disease scope is narrow (~5 abdominal conditions), external validation is from a single institution in Beijing, and several central claims — governance, expert-level performance, cross-disease generalizability — exceed what the evidence supports. He initially suggested transfer to npj Digital Medicine citing three comparable recent papers (Gaber et al. 2025, Liu et al. 2026, Jiang et al. 2025) before being persuaded by Elena Bellafante to proceed OTR, based on the argument that longitudinal self-evolution through iterative diagnostic reasoning under uncertainty is not addressed by those comparators.
System Overall Rating: Reject

Decision Recommendation
Major Revision, not Reject — but with a tight scope.
The case for revision: Both reviewers end with constructive rather than dismissive language. Reviewer 3 explicitly recommends major revision. The handling editor was persuaded to proceed OTR. The conceptual contribution — iterative evidence acquisition with a non-parametric experiential repository — is genuinely differentiated from the comparable papers cited. The ablation study structure is already partially present, meaning the authors have the experimental infrastructure to address the key gaps.
The case against (strongest counterargument): The circularity problem in the PLA cohort validation is not a presentation issue — if the DCP repository was used to shape the test data translations, the external validation result may be fundamentally unreliable. This cannot be fixed without re-running the experiment with an independent translation source. Combined with the evidence leakage concern in the example trace and the prompt-enforced workflow metrics, there are three independent validity threats that each independently could require re-evaluation of core results.
The question you should also be asking: Is the self-evolution framing salvageable, or does reframing it as supervised case-memory consolidation materially change the paper's contribution claim? If the answer is yes — that the novelty depends on the autonomous learning framing — then the revision path collapses, because Reviewer 3's critique is definitional, not empirical. The authors need to answer this explicitly before revision begins.
Bottom line: Invite major revision contingent on: (1) independent translation pipeline for the PLA cohort or acknowledgment of the circularity with bounded claims; (2) ablations distinguishing DCP retrieval from raw case retrieval and prompt-enforced versus free workflow; (3) reframing of self-evolution claims with honest characterization of the supervision signal; (4) statistical significance testing with confidence intervals per stratum. If (1) requires re-running external validation from scratch and the authors cannot do this, the paper should be rejected.
