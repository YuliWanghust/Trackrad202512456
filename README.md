Summary of Major Reviewer Concerns
Reviewer 1 (Qiao Jin) raises three structural issues. First, novelty is unclear — the paper closely parallels MetaMedQA, which already evaluates confidence scoring, missing-answer recognition, and unknown-question handling in medical LLMs. CLEAR does not sufficiently differentiate itself. Second, construct validity is weak: the perturbation design treats NA, IDK, and INA as functionally equivalent abstention outcomes, but these labels likely capture different behaviors (politeness, refusal, familiarity effects) rather than a unified "humility" construct. Third, evaluation methodology is insufficient — no comparison to log-probability or calibration-based baselines, no stronger benchmarks like MedXpertQA, and no item-level clinical adjudication to validate that abstention is actually the correct response post-perturbation.
Reviewer 2 (Jie Yang) flags data leakage risk from using datasets (MedMCQA, MedQA, JAMA CC) that have been publicly available for years and likely contaminated pre-training corpora. This is a fundamental validity threat the authors do not address. Yang also questions whether IDK/INA substitutions are valid abstention proxies — models may select these options because the correct answer is absent from the choice set, not because they lack knowledge. Additional concerns include figure clarity (overlapping boxes in Figure 1c, undefined "extraneous distractors"), undefined terminology, and English-only scope limiting generalizability.
Handling Editor (Eric Wang) independently identifies two critical methodological flaws: (1) the humility deficit metric is based solely on letter-selection rates, which cannot distinguish genuine epistemic uncertainty from prompt-induced response patterns — calibration data or log-probabilities are required to make the "inherent behavioral bias" claim credible; and (2) distractors are not stratified by semantic proximity to the ground truth, meaning a model failing against a highly plausible distractor is treated identically to failing against an implausible one. This confounds every accuracy-degradation result in the paper. Wang also notes the scaling inversion claim mixes within-family and cross-family comparisons — a conflation that undermines the interpretation.

Editorial Decision: Reject
The paper does not meet the threshold for major revision. The humility deficit construct lacks epistemic validity without calibration data — this is not a fixable data request, it undermines the paper's central behavioral claim. The distractor construction protocol confounds all accuracy-degradation results, and this cannot be remedied post-hoc. The novelty gap relative to MetaMedQA is substantive and unresolved. Three independent evaluators (two reviewers plus the handling editor) converge on the same core methodological problems. A revision would require redesigning the perturbation protocol, rerunning all experiments, and reframing the contribution — that is effectively a new paper. Reject with an invitation to resubmit if the authors rebuild the evaluation framework from the ground up.

Summary of Major Reviewer Concerns
Reviewer 1 (Lingyao Li — leaning weak rejection): The ensemble voting strategy is not novel from an LLM perspective. Performance gains over CETIS-2020-CI may simply reflect a stronger base model rather than architectural innovation. Ranking-based triage metrics are absent. The nurse experience evaluation lacks protocol detail. No discussion of ethical deployment or accountability. The GitHub repository is inadequately documented.
Reviewer 2 (Wu Seong Kang): The LLM prompting methodology is insufficiently described for reproducibility — no prompt template, no output filtering criteria, no rule acceptance criteria are provided. The rationale for choosing Random Forest over interpretable alternatives (CART, XGBoost, rule-list models) is unjustified. The overlap between CETIS-2020-CI rules and AITriage's own rule set creates a circular comparator problem. The final decision rule sets are never fully disclosed. The prospective evaluation is actually a retrospective re-triage study, and this is misrepresented in the abstract and body. The use of nurse-assigned triage as a gold standard is methodologically circular — the model is trained to replicate the same subjective judgments it claims to correct. MEWS score components overlap with individual input variables, creating potential feature leakage.
Reviewer 3 (Feng Xie — the most critical): The gold standard circularity is the most fundamental flaw: training and evaluating against nurse triage labels means the model proves only that it can approximate historical nurse behavior, not that it improves patient outcomes. CETIS-2020-CI is a potentially artificially weakened baseline because chief complaints were stripped out. Both hospital campuses belong to the same tertiary institution in Hangzhou, so the "external validation" cohort is not truly external. The prospective evaluation is mislabeled — it is a reader study, not a prospective trial. No confidence intervals are reported on any primary metric. Under-triage and over-triage rates, which carry the most clinical safety weight, are not reported. Significance asterisks appear without specifying which statistical tests were used.
Reviewer 4 (Yanwei Jin — ECR co-reviewer): Confirms the GitHub link appears to be https://github.com/Poetic-AI/ED-CITriage but could not independently verify reproducibility. Did not add substantive independent concerns beyond co-signing Reviewer 3's critique.
Handling Editor (Eric Wang): Acknowledges the hybrid LLM-as-rule-refiner architecture as principled and the ablation in Figure 2e as credible. However, flags two fatal issues explicitly: the circularity of the gold standard, and the absence of any external validity for the stated use case of rural/resource-constrained settings. Describes the paper as OTR (over the threshold for sending out) but with these concerns flagged to referees.

Editorial Decision: Major Revision, Not Rejection
Outright rejection is not warranted here. The architecture is mechanistically novel — using LLMs as rule refiners rather than direct predictors is a principled safety-aware design, and the ablation data (Random Forest 87.75% → AITriage 96.5% on the 400-case cohort) demonstrates genuine additive value. Four LLM backbones are tested, and cross-platform consistency is credible.
However, the paper cannot be accepted as submitted. The following are conditions for revision, not optional improvements.
Non-negotiable revisions required:
The gold standard circularity must be directly addressed. The authors must either validate against downstream outcome labels (ICU admission, 30-day mortality, time-to-critical intervention) on a subset of cases, or explicitly reframe all performance claims as agreement-with-nurse-practice rather than clinical correctness. The current framing is misleading and Reviewer 3 is correct that it undermines every performance claim in the paper.
The "external validation" framing must be corrected. Two campuses of the same tertiary center in Hangzhou do not constitute external validation. The authors must either acquire data from a genuinely independent site, or recharacterize the BJ cohort as an internal cross-campus replication study and remove any generalizability claims.
The full decision rule sets for all four triage levels must be disclosed in Supplementary Materials. Interpretability is a central claim of the paper; without the rules, the claim is unverifiable.
The prospective clinical evaluation must be relabeled as a reader study throughout, including in the abstract. Claims about real-time decision support must be moved to the limitations or future work sections.
95% confidence intervals must be added to all primary metrics in all results tables and figures.
The CETIS-2020-CI comparator overlap with AITriage's own embedded CETIS-derived rules must be disclosed and quantified.
If the authors cannot address the gold standard circularity with outcome-based validation data, the decision converts to rejection.

04404 Evidence Attribution
1. Overall Assessment
This manuscript presents Med-V1, a family of 3-billion-parameter models for biomedical evidence attribution, trained on MedFact-Synth — a 1.5-million-instance synthetic corpus constructed through GPT-4o-mini claim generation, MedCPT-based dense retrieval, and multi-LLM consensus labelling with GRPO-based post-training. The central claim is that 3B-parameter models can match frontier LLMs on biomedical claim verification across MedFact-Bench (five datasets; 14,274 instances), with average macro-accuracy of 0.728–0.732 versus 0.717–0.736 for frontier systems. The work addresses a genuine operational bottleneck: frontier LLM dependence for routine citation verification is unsustainable at scale. Two concerns are most consequential. First, training and inference operate exclusively on PubMed titles and abstracts; clinically important verification tasks routinely depend on full-text methodological detail, and the performance ceiling imposed by this truncation is never quantified. Second, the benchmark comparison omits the strongest directly competing compact models — MiniCheck-FT5 (770M parameters, EMNLP 2024) and Bespoke-MiniCheck-7B — which share Med-V1's synthetic data thesis and operate on overlapping benchmarks. Whether a 3B biomedical-domain model outperforms a 770M general-domain specialist is the central question the evaluation should answer but does not.

2. Strengths
MedFact-Synth's construction design is a meaningful technical advance. Decoupling claims from source articles via MedCPT retrieval produces naturalistic hard-negative pairs spanning genuine support, neutrality, and contradiction. The three-model LLM consensus achieves annotation accuracy of 0.74, exceeding individual human annotators (0.54–0.69), demonstrated with confusion matrices rather than asserted.
Architecture-agnostic generalisability strengthens the publication case. Consistent relative improvements of 27.0–71.3% across both Llama-3.2-3B and Qwen2.5-3B backbones and all five MedFact-Bench datasets demonstrate that gains are attributable to the data pipeline, not to architecture or hyperparameter selection on a single backbone.
The clinical guideline use case is the most operationally compelling component. Applying Med-V1-L3B to 57k statement-source pairs from 6,152 PubMed Central practice guideline articles and identifying 28 validated misattributions per 100 flagged cases — with 12 involving treatment effectiveness claims — positions Med-V1 as credible audit infrastructure rather than a benchmarking study, and is the finding most likely to resonate with a Nature Communications readership.
The structured error taxonomy is analytically honest. The finding that 66–71% of observed errors reflect dataset noise rather than model reasoning failures is an important corrective to benchmark over-interpretation, substantiated with sufficient qualitative evidence.

3. Weaknesses
The abstract-only constraint is the most significant limitation and is underweighted. Med-V1 verifies claims against titles and abstracts exclusively. The guideline misattribution use case — where contradicting evidence is frequently numerical and buried in body text — is directly and most consequentially affected. The authors acknowledge this but do not quantify the associated performance degradation, which would require even a small full-text held-out evaluation set.
The benchmark comparison is incomplete. Evaluating against zero-shot frontier LLMs is not the appropriate baseline for a compact domain-specialist model. MiniCheck-FT5 and Bespoke-MiniCheck-7B are the natural comparators and are absent without justification.
The hallucination use case rests on an insufficiently narrow empirical base: 40 MedAESQA questions answered by two LLMs across seven citation styles. The 96.3% PMID hallucination rate for GPT-4o and the claim that citation format strongly affects citation validity carry wide uncertainty at this sample size that is not adequately discussed.
The GRPO reward function collapses the 5-point Likert scale into a symmetric scalar, treating false support and false contradiction as equivalent errors. In clinical verification, falsely endorsing a claim the source refutes is substantially more harmful than the reverse. No ablation tests asymmetric reward weighting, and the manuscript does not acknowledge this as a limitation.

4. Editorial Decision
Send for Review, subject to mandatory revision of the benchmark comparison framework. The MedFact-Synth pipeline, dual-backbone evaluation, and guideline audit use case are publication-worthy at the Nature Communications level. Reviewers must specifically adjudicate: (i) whether exclusion of MiniCheck-FT5 and Bespoke-MiniCheck-7B is scientifically defensible; (ii) whether the hallucination use case sample size supports the claims made; and (iii) whether the symmetric GRPO reward is appropriate given the asymmetric clinical stakes of false support versus false contradiction.

5. Suggested Reviewer Expertise
Reviewers should cover: (1) compact and task-specialist NLI model development with direct experience in synthetic data generation for grounded fact verification, specifically in the LLM-AggreFact or SciFact benchmarking tradition; (2) biomedical NLP and scientific claim verification, including familiarity with MultiVerS, HealthVer, and the MiniCheck literature; (3) reinforcement learning from rule-based feedback for language model alignment, with specific exposure to GRPO in generation tasks; (4) clinical informatics and evidence-based guideline development, with expertise in how citation misattribution propagates into clinical practice; (5) PubMed-scale biomedical information retrieval and dense retrieval systems such as MedCPT.

6. State-of-the-Art Literature Review (Past 3 Years)
The most directly competing work is MiniCheck (Tang, Laban & Durrett, EMNLP 2024), which constructs GPT-4-generated synthetic training data via Claim-to-Document and Document-to-Claim procedures and fine-tunes a 770M-parameter Flan-T5 model (MiniCheck-FT5) that reaches GPT-4-level accuracy on the LLM-AggreFact benchmark at 400× lower inference cost. Bespoke-MiniCheck-7B extends this to a Llama-3.1-based 7B system representing current state-of-the-art in compact grounded fact verification across eleven pooled datasets. Med-V1 shares the same core thesis but applies it exclusively within the biomedical domain with a PubMed-grounded corpus, graded five-point verdicts, and explicit rationale generation — genuine differentiators that the current evaluation design fails to exploit by excluding these models from the comparison table. MedRAGChecker (Fu et al., 2025, ACL) presents a claim-level verification pipeline for biomedical RAG outputs that fuses NLI-based semantic entailment with knowledge-graph-grounded verification, distilled into a compact student verifier; its hybrid architecture and claim-level evaluation methodology are directly analogous to Med-V1's deployment context and should be engaged with explicitly. SourceCheckup (Wu et al., Nature Communications, April 2025) audits citation validity in LLM-generated medical answers at scale, finding that API-only models produce valid source URLs only 40–70% of the time; this work addresses the same public-health stakes as Med-V1's guideline audit use case and provides a natural framing comparator. MedCite (ACL Findings 2025) proposes a framework for generating biomedical answers with verified per-sentence citations using retrieval-reranking and NLI, evaluated on BioASQ and PubMedQA — a closely analogous task operationally, though from the generation rather than verification side. Liang & Sonntag (BioNLP Workshop, ACL 2025) advance structured four-step prompting for biomedical claim verification using general-purpose LLMs, providing a prompt-engineering baseline that complements Med-V1's fine-tuning approach on the same benchmark tasks. Finally, the TREC BioGen 2024–2025 track (Demner-Fushman et al., 2025) operationalises per-sentence citation attribution grounded in PubMed at competition scale, establishing a community benchmark against which Med-V1's claim-attribution capabilities — particularly in the hallucination use case — should be contextualised.

Additional Literature Review: 5–6 Papers Sharing Similar Scope
1. MiniCheck: Efficient Fact-Checking of LLMs on Grounding Documents
Tang, Laban & Durrett — EMNLP 2024. Trains compact fact-checking models (770M Flan-T5, 7B Llama-3.1) on GPT-4-generated synthetic data using Claim-to-Document and Document-to-Claim procedures, achieving GPT-4-level accuracy on the unified LLM-AggreFact benchmark at 400× lower cost. This is the closest methodological parallel to Med-V1: same synthetic-data-to-small-model thesis, overlapping benchmark tasks. The key differentiator is that MiniCheck operates domain-agnostically and produces binary verdicts without rationales, whereas Med-V1 is biomedical-specific and outputs five-point graded verdicts with natural-language explanations. Med-V1's failure to benchmark against MiniCheck-FT5 is the single most consequential gap in the evaluation design.
2. SourceCheckup: An Automated Framework for Assessing How Well LLMs Cite Relevant Medical References
Wu, Wu et al. — Nature Communications, April 2025. Constructs an evaluation pipeline that audits citation validity in LLM-generated medical answers at scale, finding that API-only models produce valid source URLs only 40–70% of the time and that RAG-enabled models substantially reduce URL hallucination. Directly parallels Med-V1's hallucination use case in motivation and clinical stakes, and was published in the same journal. Where SourceCheckup evaluates whether citations exist and are reachable, Med-V1 evaluates whether reachable citations actually support the claim — the two works are complementary and should be read together. Authors should cite and differentiate from this work explicitly.
3. MedRAGChecker: Claim-Level Verification for Biomedical RAG Outputs
Fu et al. — ACL 2025. Proposes a hybrid verification pipeline that fuses NLI-based semantic entailment with knowledge-graph-grounded checking, distilled into a compact student verifier for claim-level factuality assessment in biomedical RAG systems. The claim-level granularity, compact model distillation strategy, and PubMed-grounded evaluation overlap substantially with Med-V1's inference pipeline and guideline audit methodology. Unlike Med-V1, MedRAGChecker incorporates structured biomedical knowledge constraints as a complementary signal to surface errors that surface-form NLI alone misses — a design choice Med-V1 does not discuss or compare against.
4. EvidenceMap: Learning Evidence Analysis to Unleash the Power of Small Language Models for Biomedical Question Answering
Zong et al. — arXiv 2025 (accepted, Artificial Intelligence in Medicine). Fine-tunes a 66M-parameter model to perform multifaceted evidence analysis — supportive evaluation, logical correlation, content summarisation — over PubMed-retrieved snippets, guiding a 3B generative model. Achieves performance exceeding a RAG pipeline with an 8B LLM by 19.9% and 5.7% on BioASQ and PubMedQA. Directly comparable in the small-model-for-biomedical-evidence paradigm; the distinction is that EvidenceMap targets answer generation rather than claim verification, and uses a decomposed analysis architecture rather than end-to-end post-training. The shared 3B scale and PubMed evidence sourcing make direct performance comparison informative.
5. Advancing Biomedical Claim Verification by Using Large Language Models with Better Structured Prompting Strategies
Liang & Sonntag — BioNLP Workshop, ACL 2025. Proposes a structured four-step prompting strategy (CoENLI) that guides LLMs through evidence-based rationale generation before arriving at a final NLI classification, evaluated on SciFact and HealthVer benchmarks — two of the five datasets in Med-V1's MedFact-Bench. This is the strongest prompt-engineering baseline in the biomedical claim verification space and provides a direct methodological foil: Med-V1 uses task-specific post-training; Liang & Sonntag achieve competitive performance through structured prompting alone. The manuscript should address whether Med-V1's gains over frontier LLMs persist when those LLMs are prompted with structured CoENLI-style reasoning rather than evaluated zero-shot.
6. MedCite: Can Language Models Generate Verifiable Text with Citations?
ACL Findings 2025. Proposes an end-to-end framework for generating biomedical answers with per-sentence, retrieval-grounded citations using hierarchical retrieval and NLI-based reranking, evaluated on BioASQ and PubMedQA. Whereas Med-V1 operates as a post-hoc verifier — taking an existing claim-source pair and scoring it — MedCite integrates citation generation and verification into a single pipeline. The two architectures represent complementary deployment paradigms (auditing versus generation-with-attribution), and MedCite's NLI reranking component is functionally analogous to Med-V1's inference stage. Together these papers define the current frontier for PubMed-grounded claim attribution and should be explicitly positioned against one another.

042777 Clinical Response

Section 1: Overall Assessment
This manuscript presents a post hoc mechanistic analysis of a sham-controlled RCT (KCT0009493; n=93 per-protocol) of a mobile DTx for TMD. The central claim is that OBC-defined behavioural modification — rather than app engagement volume — mediates approximately 29% of the total VAS treatment effect, with week 4 identified as a clinically actionable decision node and baseline PHQ-4 ≥3 as a modest response moderator.
The conceptual contribution — empirically distinguishing consumed dose from realised dose within a sham-controlled trial — is timely and clinically relevant. However, the per-protocol sample of n=93, with the mediation pathway tested on n=44 and the OBC modifier subgroup reduced to n=21, is critically underpowered for the number and complexity of secondary analyses performed. The PHQ-4 moderator interaction loses significance when modelled continuously (p=0.057), directly undermining the binary model result (p=0.04), a material inconsistency the authors do not adequately resolve.

Section 2: Strengths
The triangulation across three independent measurement modalities — self-reported OBC, objective server-log engagement data, and clinical VAS outcomes — is rigorous. OBC modifiers achieved significantly greater pain reduction (−45.71 vs −22.61 mm; p<0.01) with no corresponding difference across any engagement metric, providing convergent evidence against usage quantity as a therapeutic surrogate.
The causal mediation framework under the potential outcomes framework with bootstrap-based 95% CIs (1,000 iterations) is methodologically appropriate, and the explicit framing of all results as associative reflects discipline rarely maintained in post hoc analyses.
The week-4 sensitivity analysis adds genuine clinical value, with treatment ORs 33–42% higher than at the 6-week endpoint and OBC predictive significance emerging at the ≥70% threshold by week 4 but absent at week 2.

Section 3: Weaknesses
The per-protocol sample is inadequate for the analytic ambitions of this paper. Mediation analysis on n=44, moderation on an unbalanced PHQ-4 split (65 high vs 28 low), and subgroup comparisons between n=21 and n=23 are underpowered throughout. The PHQ-4 moderator instability across continuous versus binary specification is unresolved and renders that claim unreliable.
The OBC modifier classification — a post hoc median split within the DTx arm alone — cannot distinguish whether behavioural modification precedes pain reduction or whether both are driven by unmeasured confounders such as motivation or treatment expectation. No analysis links OBC modification to specific app content modules, leaving the mechanism claim partially unsupported.

Section 4: Editorial Decision
Reject. The sample size is critically insufficient for the number and complexity of secondary analyses performed, the PHQ-4 moderator finding is statistically unstable, and the OBC median-split construct does not support the causal framing used in the discussion. This work is better positioned at npj Digital Medicine or JMIR mHealth and uHealth.

Section 5: Suggested Reviewer Expertise
Reviewers should include expertise in: (1) causal mediation analysis under the potential outcomes framework, specifically for non-randomised mediators; (2) statistical methodology for post hoc RCT secondary analyses, including power constraints and exploratory moderator model interpretation; (3) digital therapeutics design and engagement science for app-based chronic musculoskeletal interventions; (4) TMD clinical management and DC/TMD Axis II instruments, including the psychometric properties of the OBC and PHQ-4 in orofacial pain populations; and (5) chronic pain psychology and behavioural habit formation models applied to self-management digital interventions.

Section 6: State-of-the-Art Literature Review
The DTx mechanism-of-action field has gained conceptual and empirical momentum over the past three years. Schmidt et al. (npj Digital Medicine, 2026) formalised the distinction between consumed dose — patient interaction with a DTx — and realised dose — the degree to which cognitive-affective and behavioural change is actually internalised — arguing that clinical outcomes are driven by dose realisation rather than dose intake alone. This paper directly operationalises that framework in a sham-controlled RCT context, which is its primary differentiator. The parent trial (Park et al., JMIR, 2025) established efficacy of the same Clickless DTx TMD-01 intervention at 6 weeks across VAS, MMO, and JFLS-20 outcomes, making the present manuscript a second-generation mechanistic elaboration of that dataset — a relationship that raises questions about whether this constitutes a sufficiently independent contribution or an analytical extension better integrated into the parent publication. Cheng et al. (JMIR Formative Research, 2023) examined behavioural mechanisms mediating health improvements from a digital intervention for chronic musculoskeletal pain, identifying behavioural activation and pain acceptance as partial mediators, providing a methodologically comparable precedent without a sham-controlled comparator. A pilot RCT (n=40) published in the Journal of Dentistry (2024) demonstrated DTx-driven pain and mouth-opening improvements in TMD but without significant between-group differences in OBC, contextualising the present manuscript's focus on OBC change magnitude rather than group-level OBC response. The present manuscript sits at the intersection of this emerging mechanistic literature but does not yet command the sample size or analytical rigour to establish the mediation pathway as definitively as the discussion implies. 043599 1. Overall Assessment
DeepArrest proposes a federated learning framework for in-hospital cardiac arrest (IHCA) prediction combining a temporal convolutional network (TCN) for six-vital-sign time series and XGBoost for static EMR features, with outputs fused via logistic regression. Validation spans four Taiwanese hospitals (34,381 patients, 1,184 IHCA cases), with TCN components externally evaluated on MIMIC-IV and eICU-CRD. The headline claim is IHCA prediction up to six hours in advance using only routinely available vital signs, with recall improvements up to 17 percentage points over local baselines.
The multi-site federated architecture and the constraint to laboratory-free inputs address real clinical deployment barriers. However, the technical contribution is incremental: TCNs, FedProx, XGBoost, SMOTE, and SHAP are each established methods applied without meaningful architectural novelty. More critically, the evaluation reports only recall and AUROC — precision, positive predictive value, and calibration are entirely absent. A recall exceeding 96% without a corresponding false positive rate is clinically uninterpretable and operationally misleading given nursing alert-fatigue constraints. This omission is not a minor revision; it undermines the central clinical utility claim.

2. Strengths
The four-hospital federated design with markedly heterogeneous IHCA ratios (approximately 0.03 at NTUH to 0.05 at CGMH) provides a realistic test of cross-site generalizability that single-center studies cannot replicate. The deliberate restriction to six routinely acquirable vital signs directly addresses eCART's known deployment bottleneck, and the paper makes this design rationale internally consistent.
The ablation structure comparing single-track versus dual-track performance across 1-, 3-, and 6-hour prediction horizons is methodologically sound. The finding that TCN alone saturates near 100% at short horizons while the dual-track system maintains advantage at 12 hours is clinically meaningful and avoids conflating near-event detection with actionable early warning.
The SHAP analysis of the XGBoost lower track produces institution-specific feature importance rankings that appropriately acknowledge heterogeneity in clinical practice. The consistent emergence of oxygen supplementation, AVPU, and arrhythmia as dominant predictors across sites is clinically plausible and supports model transparency.

3. Weaknesses
The evaluation reports only recall and AUROC. Precision, PPV, F1, and calibration curves are absent. At the reported recall levels (>96%), any realistic false positive rate in a 1:28 imbalanced dataset implies an operationally unsustainable alarm burden. The paper cannot demonstrate clinical utility without this information.
The claim that pretraining the TCN on all-cause mortality from MIMIC-IV provides generalizable cardiac arrest representations is central to the upper-track design but is unsupported. Natural death and IHCA are physiologically distinct; the authors cite observational vital sign overlap but provide no domain adaptation analysis, feature distribution similarity metrics, or ablation isolating the pretraining contribution.
The FedProx convergence anomaly at CSH — the most class-imbalanced site, where the local model unexpectedly outperforms the federated model — is acknowledged but not investigated. This is precisely the scenario where FedProx's theoretical guarantees should apply most forcefully. No confidence intervals or statistical significance tests are reported for any metric, rendering all FL algorithm comparisons unverifiable.

4. Editorial Decision
Reject. The absence of precision, PPV, and calibration reporting constitutes a structural evaluation failure that cannot be addressed through minor revision. 043667 Forgetting Reduction"

1. Overall Assessment
This manuscript proposes Sleep-CLS, a biologically inspired continual learning framework for class-incremental classification that emulates wake–sleep alternation to mitigate catastrophic forgetting across natural and medical image datasets. The framework employs LIF spiking dynamics, Poisson-spike replay from per-task mean prototypes, k-WTA competition, and unsupervised Hebbian plasticity—achieving consolidation without task identifiers or stored raw samples. The internal consistency of the system design is a genuine strength, and cross-domain medical evaluation spanning endoscopy, OCT, histopathology, and blood cell microscopy is substantively broader than most comparable work.
Two concerns dominate the assessment. First, the experimental scope is structurally insufficient: all medical evaluations use only 3–4 classes and exactly two sequential tasks on 300-sample-per-class subsets, rendering the continual learning scenario far simpler than any plausible clinical deployment. No evidence is provided for performance under longer task sequences, where the single-mean-vector prototype assumption is most likely to fail under intra-class heterogeneity. Second, novelty differentiation from SRC (ref. 43)—from which the core architecture is directly derived—and from the concurrent WSCL (Sorrenti et al., IEEE TNNLS 2024) and SESLR (arXiv:2507.02901, 2025) frameworks is inadequately established. Both share CLS theory motivation, wake-sleep alternation, and overlapping benchmark datasets.

2. Strengths
The task-agnostic, memory-free consolidation mechanism addresses a genuine and practically meaningful constraint. Operating without task identifiers or stored raw samples directly satisfies GDPR and HIPAA deployment constraints, and the per-task mean vector representation scales as O(d·T) rather than O(n·T). The justification for this sufficiency—that frozen VGG features produce approximately Gaussian, unimodal clusters amenable to prototype-based replay—is theoretically coherent and empirically validated through the non-overlapping sample ablation in Table 2.
The ablation study (Section "Lesion Experiments," Fig. 5) is the manuscript's strongest analytical contribution. Independently removing k-WTA and the REM-like phase across all eight medical datasets establishes that these components have functionally distinct roles—task-specific stabilisation versus cross-task integration—and that their combination yields synergistic gains (e.g., BloodMNIST-3: 75.78% → 96.78%). This is a necessary experiment that biologically inspired papers routinely omit.
The Grad-CAM and prototype correlation analyses (Figs. 2–4, Table 3) provide genuine mechanistic evidence rather than post-hoc rationalisation. The near-complete orthogonalisation of class-prototype correlation matrices after REM (off-diagonal |ρ| ≤ 0.09 vs. 0.66–0.90 pre-sleep in Retina-OCT-4) quantitatively supports the claim that the sleep phase separates representational subspaces, not merely adjusts decision thresholds.

3. Weaknesses
The two-task, 3–4 class experimental design is the central structural weakness. Class-incremental learning in clinical settings may require sequences of 10–20+ pathology categories over multi-year deployment windows. The single mean vector per task is explicitly acknowledged to fail under complex intra-class distributions (lines 440–444), yet no experiment tests this failure mode or attempts sub-prototyping. The claims of generalisability therefore rest on the easiest plausible instantiation of the problem.
The frozen VGG-16 backbone is an unresolved confound. All classification is effectively performed in a fixed 25,088-dimensional ImageNet-pretrained feature space; the sleep mechanism reorganises a representation it did not learn. No end-to-end or partially unfrozen backbone experiment is included. It remains unclear whether the reported gains are attributable to Sleep-CLS or to the discriminative quality of VGG features alone.
The privacy preservation claim is asserted without formal substantiation. No differential privacy analysis, membership inference experiment, or mutual information bound on image reconstruction from stored mean vectors is provided. The statement that mean vectors "do not directly permit reconstruction of raw images" is plausible but unproven. For a journal at this level, privacy claims require grounding in the formal privacy literature.

4. Editorial Decision
Reject. The manuscript presents a technically coherent and internally consistent system, but the experimental scope does not support the central claims at the level required by this journal. The restriction to two sequential tasks and small class subsets represents a fundamental limitation requiring new experiments, not revision of existing ones. The novelty differentiation from WSCL (Sorrenti et al., 2024) and SESLR (2025) is insufficient, and privacy claims are unsubstantiated. The authors should substantially expand task-sequence length, include end-to-end backbone ablations, provide formal privacy analysis, and directly benchmark against the most proximal competing frameworks before resubmission. Transfer to npj Digital Medicine or Communications Engineering is recommended if the medical framing is retained at the current experimental scale.

5. Suggested Reviewer Expertise
Peer reviewers should be drawn from the following areas: (1) continual learning and class-incremental learning theory, with specific familiarity with SNN-based and biologically inspired replay mechanisms; (2) spiking neural network architectures and neuromorphic computing, including LIF dynamics and Hebbian plasticity rules in the context of classification tasks; (3) medical image analysis with experience in multi-modal classification across endoscopy, OCT, and histopathology datasets; (4) formal privacy and trustworthy AI, including differential privacy, federated learning privacy guarantees, and membership inference attacks; and (5) computational neuroscience, specifically sleep-dependent memory consolidation and hippocampal–cortical CLS models, to assess the biological fidelity claims.

043788 Medical Dialogue Agent

1. Overall Assessment
This manuscript introduces BeTA, an 8-billion-parameter medical dialogue agent that maintains an explicit categorical belief over four patient communication types (Normal, Chaos, Concealing, Both) and conditions turn-level questioning strategy on that belief. The central claim is that this design achieves Diag-Acc 0.426 in 4.9 turns on NSMT-MedDiag, reducing dialogue turns by 44% relative to DoctorAgent-RL (0.404 in 8.7 turns) while closing the Normal–NS performance gap to 0.010. The authors also introduce NSMT-MedDiag, a 10,518-episode benchmark annotated for non-standard communication type.
The contribution is credible. Addressing patient-side communication variability is a legitimate and underserved gap. The dominant concern is that training, patient simulation, and test evaluation all derive from the same GPT-4o-translated Mandarin Chinese corpora, creating a circularity that the authors acknowledge but do not quantify sufficiently. The second concern is that Diag-Acc is a text-similarity composite rather than a clinically adjudicated diagnostic correctness measure, which limits the validity of clinical claims.

2. Strengths
Framing patient communication variability as an explicit latent variable to be inferred — rather than a nuisance to be averaged over — is conceptually distinctive. The RICO structured output, decomposing each turn into a reasoning trace, belief distribution, anticipated response category, and strategy-conditioned question, provides an interpretable interface absent in all frontier baselines. The learned belief-to-strategy mapping independently recapitulates motivational interviewing principles without hand-coding, providing construct validity.
NSMT-MedDiag is a genuine dataset contribution. Disease-disjoint splits across 711 diseases from four independent corpora, with turn-level communication-type annotation and a separately validated patient simulator (CSF 0.875, DSA 0.912, CTA 0.907), represent a methodologically sound evaluation infrastructure not previously available to the field.
The five-rung cumulative ablation localises more than 94% of the accuracy gain to the single C4→A8 transition and identifies CL5 NS-reward as a fairness regulariser rather than an accuracy driver. This decomposition is cleaner than most RL-for-dialogue ablations. Blinded physician evaluation on 200 episodes with κ = 0.72 inter-rater agreement and McNemar testing adds meaningful external validity.

3. Weaknesses
The patient simulator and doctor agent share the same data provenance — both trained on GPT-4o translations of four Chinese corpora. OOD degradation figures (−0.018, −0.024) remain within the same translated-Chinese distribution. No evaluation on English-native corpora, standardised patient actors, or real clinical interactions is provided. The Discussion claim that BeTA is "clinically faithful in real-world clinical settings" is unsupported.
Diag-Acc is a composite of ROUGE-L and BERTScore-F1 on predicted-diagnosis text, not a clinically adjudicated endpoint. A score of 0.426 conveys no direct clinical meaning, and the physician evaluation covers communication quality, not diagnostic accuracy. The gap between these two forms of validity is not bridged.
No demographic subgroup analysis is presented. The four communication types plausibly correlate with patient demographic characteristics — Concealing behaviour is well-documented in stigmatised conditions and marginalised populations — and systematic belief-accuracy or strategy-selection differences across subgroups are not examined. This omission is material given the equity framing.

4. Editorial Decision
Send for Review, with reservations. The belief-guided RL framework, NSMT-MedDiag benchmark, and curriculum design are sufficiently novel to warrant peer adjudication. Reviewers should be asked specifically to evaluate: (1) whether simulator-circularity constitutes a fundamental validity threat; (2) whether Diag-Acc is an adequate primary endpoint or whether clinician-adjudicated correctness is required; (3) whether the absence of demographic subgroup analysis is acceptable; and (4) whether comparative language regarding frontier LLM performance is adequately qualified given the zero-shot evaluation condition applied to those baselines.

5. Suggested Reviewer Expertise
Reviewers should include expertise across: GRPO and curriculum reinforcement learning for structured language generation; transformer-based belief-state tracking and partially observable sequential decision-making; automated medical dialogue evaluation methodology, including simulator validity and clinical metric design; primary care or internal medicine clinicians with experience in standardised patient evaluation and motivational interviewing; and researchers specialising in demographic bias and health equity in clinical AI systems.

6. State-of-the-Art Literature Review
The automated clinical history-taking field has advanced substantially since 2023, with AMIE (Tu et al., Nature 2025) as the landmark reference. Built on PaLM 2 via self-play simulated dialogue, AMIE exceeded primary care physicians on 28 of 32 evaluation axes in a randomised crossover OSCE study — setting a translational benchmark that simulator-only evaluations must now explicitly position against. A multimodal AMIE extension (2025, Gemini 2.0 Flash) further demonstrated superiority on 7 of 9 multimodal axes. DoctorAgent-RL (Feng et al., ICASSP 2026), BeTA's closest direct comparator, applies GRPO in a multi-agent collaborative framework but treats patient communication style as fixed input. Doctor-R1 (2025) extends this line with a two-tiered empathy-and-outcome reward architecture and experiential agentic RL, also without modelling patient communication variability. MedRIA (Zou et al., JMIR 2024) explores RL-based inquiry efficiency in emergency settings using actor-critic frameworks but requires manual feature engineering. PaMis (Zhao et al., 2024) detects and mitigates patient misreport via dialogue entity graphs and graph entropy, addressing a partially overlapping problem from a generation rather than RL perspective. MedDialBench (Luo et al., 2025 preprint — notably co-authored by Xun Jiang, a corresponding author on the BeTA manuscript) introduces parametric adversarial patient behaviour with dose-response characterisation, showing worst-case diagnostic accuracy drops of 38–54 percentage points under non-cooperative patients; this work directly supports BeTA's motivating problem and should be prominently cited and distinguished. BeTA advances this landscape by being the first to train and evaluate a policy conditioned on inferred communication type; it does not advance real-world translation, multimodal capability, or demographic equity analysis, which remain open gaps.
