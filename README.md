043111 On-Device Deployment

### 1. Overall Assessment

This manuscript presents Paper2Pulse, an end-to-end pipeline converting smartphone photographs of paper ECGs into digitized signals and diagnostic predictions, with fully on-device iOS deployment. The central claim is that robust digitization is the principal bottleneck for accurate cardiac diagnosis from photographed ECGs, and that the proposed three-step architecture — keypoint detection, Thin-Plate Spline grid rectification, and soft-argmax signal extraction — resolves this bottleneck at state-of-the-art performance. The clinical motivation is genuine and underexplored. The work is technically complete, with open code, a novel dataset (ECG-Paper-1K), and a deployed application. Two concerns govern this assessment: clinical validation is structurally inadequate, and the diagnostic scope substantially limits generalizability claims.

### 2. Strengths

The digitization pipeline addresses a specific, technically difficult failure mode: camera-captured photographs with staining, mold, folds, and perspective distortion. The ablation in Table 3 rigorously quantifies each stage's contribution — removing keypoint detection drops SNR to 9.51 dB; removing grid rectification collapses performance to −17.03 dB — establishing that the architecture is not overengineered.

ECG-Paper-1K is a meaningful dataset contribution: 1,000 smartphone photographs with controlled real-world artifacts, paired ground-truth digital signals, and diagnostic labels from a held-out PTB-XL subset constructed to prevent leakage. This is, to the authors' knowledge, the only publicly available dataset of mobile phone ECG photographs with both digitization ground truth and diagnostic classification labels.

The core empirical finding — signal-based classifiers on digitized outputs achieve F1=0.718 versus F1=0.239 for the best image-based method on real photographs — is well-supported across Table 2 and consistent across PTB-XL, CODE-15%, and ECG-Paper-1K, establishing this as a structural property of the approach.

The on-device iOS deployment, processing a complete pipeline in ~39 seconds on an iPhone 17 Pro without server dependency, with CSV export capability for EHR integration, addresses real deployment barriers that cloud-dependent alternatives do not.

### 3. Weaknesses

The clinical validation is the paper's most significant weakness. The usability study (N=16) involves biomedical informatics researchers, not cardiologists or frontline clinical users in LMIC settings — the paper's stated target population. There is no comparison against cardiologist interpretation as a reference standard, no diagnostic agreement measurement, and no data on performance in the rural hospitals and mobile tele-cardiology networks invoked throughout. A System Usability Scale score from domain experts does not substitute for clinical evaluation.

The diagnostic scope excludes the rhythm disorders most clinically relevant in acute resource-limited settings. Classification covers five coarse PTB-XL superclass categories (NORM, MI, STTC, CD, HYP). Atrial fibrillation, complete heart block, and ventricular tachycardia — conditions most likely to present acutely in low-infrastructure environments — are absent.

ECG-Paper-1K was constructed under controlled conditions using a single Samsung S24 device on standardised letter-sized paper, which is substantially more homogeneous than the clinical archives described in the introduction. 1,000 images is insufficient to characterise performance across the demographic and geographic diversity required for the global deployment claims in the abstract.

The paper does not discuss regulatory considerations. A pipeline outputting multi-label diagnostic predictions from smartphone photographs — deployed as a consumer iOS application with no uncertainty quantification, no failure detection, and no defined intended operator — has direct FDA Software as a Medical Device implications that are entirely unaddressed.

### 4. Editorial Decision

**Reject.** The manuscript presents genuine technical contributions but the clinical validation is structurally inadequate for a *Nature Communications* Digital Health submission. The usability study with domain-expert researchers does not substitute for clinical evaluation; the diagnostic scope excludes the rhythm disorders most clinically relevant in resource-limited acute settings; and the deployment framing requires regulatory discussion that is entirely absent. These gaps cannot be resolved through revision without substantial new data collection and prospective clinical evaluation. Transfer to *npj Digital Medicine* or *JMIR mHealth and uHealth* is recommended, as both journals routinely handle technically strong systems papers with preliminary deployment evidence at this clinical validation level.

### 6. State-of-the-Art Literature Review (Past 3 Years)

The ECG digitization landscape has advanced substantially since 2023, with the 2024 George B. Moody PhysioNet Challenge serving as the principal community benchmark. The PhysioNet 2024 challenge (Reyna et al., CinC 2024) formalized the task and introduced ECG-Image-Kit alongside a large-scale synthetic-to-real evaluation framework, providing the first shared benchmark for cross-team comparison. The winning digitization approach (Krones et al., arXiv:2410.14185) combines Hough Transform-based line detection with deep learning segmentation using nnU-Net, achieving first place in the digitization task and establishing the current state-of-the-art baseline that Paper2Pulse directly competes against. A recent parallel contribution from Demolder et al. (*J. Electrocardiol.* 2025) describes a high-precision smartphone-based digitization system evaluated on a large independent database including perspective-distorted images, reaching SNR of approximately 19.65 dB on scanned ECGs with common artifacts. A further open-source framework from Ranjbar et al., published in *npj Digital Medicine* in January 2026, addresses digitization at scale on 37,191 ECG images including the Emory Paper Digitization ECG Dataset (35,595 images), reporting improvements over Krones et al. across all subgroups.

Paper2Pulse competes credibly on digitization SNR — particularly on severely degraded real-world photographs where prior methods collapse — and the integration of digitization with signal-based classification is the paper's most distinctive contribution relative to this landscape. What the paper largely sidesteps, however, is engagement with concurrent work that shares its end-to-end framing. The DigitHeart system (Automatic Detection of Cardiac Conditions from Photos of ECG Captured by Smartphones, medRxiv 2023) addresses a nearly identical clinical problem — waveform extraction from smartphone photographs followed by ML-based diagnosis — with a similar three-stage architecture and comparable real-world testing, yet is not cited or compared against. The authors should have engaged directly with DigitHeart and the Ranjbar et al. *npj Digital Medicine* paper to position their contribution accurately.

### Additional Literature Review: Papers Sharing Similar Scope (Past 3 Years)

**1. Krones et al., "Combining Hough Transform and Deep Learning Approaches to Reconstruct ECG Signals From Printouts," arXiv:2410.14185, 2024.**
The PhysioNet 2024 Challenge-winning digitization method. It combines classical Hough Transform line detection with nnU-Net segmentation to reconstruct 12-lead ECG signals from printouts across diverse degradation conditions. Trained on synthetic ECG-Image-Kit data and evaluated on the PhysioNet 2024 hidden test set, it is the direct methodological competitor to Paper2Pulse-D. Unlike Paper2Pulse, it does not address the downstream classification task, does not deploy on-device, and was not evaluated on camera-captured smartphone photographs with the severity of real-world degradation present in ECG-Paper-1K.

**2. Demolder et al., "High Precision ECG Digitization Using Artificial Intelligence," *Journal of Electrocardiology*, 90:153900, 2025.**
A fully automated, smartphone-based digitization pipeline from Powerful Medical (PMcardio), evaluated on a large independent database with real-world imaging challenges including perspective distortion, lighting variation, and overlapping signals. It achieves high SNR on scanned papers and demonstrates clinical parameter preservation. The scope is digitization only — no downstream diagnostic classification is reported, and no on-device deployment or dataset release accompanies the paper, limiting reproducibility compared to Paper2Pulse.

**3. Ranjbar et al., "Digitizing Paper ECGs at Scale: An Open-Source Algorithm for Clinical Research," *npj Digital Medicine*, 2026.**
A fully automated, open-source modular framework validated on 37,191 ECG images, including 1,596 from Akershus University Hospital and the 35,595-image Emory Paper Digitization ECG Dataset. It achieves a mean SNR of 19.65 dB on scanned papers with common artifacts and outperforms Krones et al. across all subcategories. This paper was published after the Paper2Pulse submission window but represents the closest large-scale institutional validation effort in the field. Paper2Pulse's advantage remains the coupled digitize-and-classify architecture and the smartphone photograph use case, which this framework does not address.

**4. DigitHeart — "Automatic Detection of Cardiac Conditions from Photos of ECG Captured by Smartphones," medRxiv, 2023.**
A cloud-based smartphone application employing a three-stage pipeline — object detection for waveform localization, image segmentation for gridline removal, and scale calibration for voltage-time series extraction — followed by ML-based diagnostic classification. The clinical scope and deployment context are near-identical to Paper2Pulse, covering multi-label diagnosis from photographed ECGs in low-infrastructure settings. This paper is not cited or compared against in Paper2Pulse, which is a significant omission. The key differentiator is on-device versus cloud execution and the scale of the real-world evaluation dataset.

**5. Wang et al., "A Deep Learning Pipeline Using Synthetic Data to Improve Interpretation of Paper ECG Images," arXiv:2507.21968, 2025.**
The winning entry to the 2024 British Heart Foundation Open Data Science Challenge, proposing a ConvNeXt-based two-stage fine-tuning strategy on synthetic and external ECG image datasets for direct image-based classification of paper ECGs into five diagnostic categories (AUROC 0.969 on the public test set). This paper directly challenges Paper2Pulse's core premise: it achieves competitive diagnostic performance without a digitization intermediary, relying instead on robust image-level pre-training and noise reduction preprocessing. The authors of Paper2Pulse claim a structural advantage for the digitize-then-classify paradigm, but this work suggests that sufficiently pre-trained image-based models may close that gap on cleaner image inputs.

**6. Chou and González, "Dual Deep Learning System to Digitize and Classify 12-Lead ECGs from Scanned Images," CinC 2024.**
A joint digitization-and-classification pipeline submitted to the PhysioNet 2024 Challenge, combining a YOLOv7-based lead detection module with a CNN with self-attention for signal reconstruction, and an EfficientNet-B0 with sample weighting for 11-label multi-class diagnosis. Trained on 336,000 synthetic images, it is the most architecturally analogous competing system to Paper2Pulse in the PhysioNet 2024 field. Its internal SNR of 1.479 falls far below Paper2Pulse-D's real-world performance, confirming that the TPS-based rectification and keypoint normalization in Paper2Pulse confer a meaningful signal quality advantage. However, evaluation was confined to the PhysioNet synthetic distribution, and real-world smartphone photograph performance was not assessed.


042871 Injury

### 1. Overall Assessment

This manuscript proposes a composite biomarker — TyG-HGB (TyG × hemoglobin) — as an independent predictor of high altitude-induced organ injury (HAOI), and constructs a random forest (RF) classifier trained in 524 participants and externally validated in 305 participants from two Chinese PLA hospitals. The central claim is that TyG-HGB independently predicts HAOI (adjusted OR 1.48 per 1-SD; external AUC 0.803), surpasses its constituent markers individually, and anchors an RF model achieving AUC 0.906 internally.

The clinical question is legitimate. HAOI screening tools remain sparse, and combining metabolic dysfunction with hypoxia-driven erythrocytosis as a composite signal is physiologically coherent. However, the study has structural generalizability problems that cannot be resolved without new data. The development cohort is 100% male with a mean age of 24.85 years, drawn exclusively from PLA military units; the validation cohort is 97.7% male with equivalent demographics. This eliminates any claim of generalizability to the broader high-altitude population — including civilians, women, and older permanent residents explicitly invoked in the introduction. The RF model's AUC drops from 0.906 (internal) to 0.803 (external) without formal statistical interrogation. These two constraints together make the paper's framing undefendable at this journal's standard.

### 2. Strengths

The two-cohort design with geographically and temporally separated development (Xinjiang, 2023–2024) and validation (Qinghai, 2025) cohorts exceeds the standard of most comparable HAOI studies, which rely on single-institution designs. Temporal separation reduces retrospective leakage risk across cohort boundaries.

The analytical pipeline is complete: RCS with four knots characterizes a J-shaped dose-response with a threshold inflection at TyG-HGB 1804.19; three incremental multivariable regression models quantify confounder-adjusted associations; and reclassification metrics (NRI 0.4449, IDI 0.0505, both P < 0.001) formally demonstrate the index's incremental contribution beyond established risk factors.

The quasi-Bayesian Monte Carlo mediation analysis (1,000 simulations) identifying AIP as accounting for 30.86% of the TyG-HGB → HAOI association is a genuine mechanistic contribution, situating lipid dysregulation as the primary causal mediator and moving the work beyond pure prediction.

### 3. Weaknesses

The study population is demographically near-homogeneous — young, male, and exclusively PLA military — which forecloses any claim of generalizability to the 81 million permanent high-altitude residents the introduction invokes. The outcome definitions (e.g., LDH >240 U/L, CK-MB >25 U/L) will differ in clinical significance across age groups, sexes, and comorbidity profiles. This is not a minor caveat — it fundamentally limits what the paper can claim.

The RF model's AUC declines 11 points (0.906 → 0.803) externally. This gap is not tested via a DeLong comparison of internal versus external AUCs, and calibration degradation (Brier 0.10 → 0.184) is described as "robust" without quantifying the clinical cost of 71 false negatives in 305 patients. A formal performance heterogeneity analysis is required before external validity can be asserted.

The TyG-HGB composite (TyG × HGB) lacks derivation justification. No interaction testing or comparison against functional alternatives (TyG/HGB, TyG + HGB as a linear term) is reported. The 22-percentage-point HAOI incidence gap between cohorts (68% vs. 46%) is unexplained and likely reflects cohort-level confounding that inflates apparent internal performance.

The DeepSeek-V3-integrated clinical decision support system receives no technical evaluation — no prompt methodology, no hallucination risk assessment, and no regulatory analysis. This section is scientifically indefensible as presented.

### 4. Editorial Decision

**Reject.** The manuscript does not meet the bar for *Nature Communications*. The demographically homogeneous population forecloses the generalizability claims central to the paper's framing; the AUC performance gap is statistically unaddressed; the composite index lacks functional form justification; and the LLM-based CDSS component is unevaluated. These flaws are not revision-addressable within the existing dataset. Transfer to *High Altitude Medicine & Biology* or *npj Digital Medicine* is recommended after broadening the study population across sex and age strata and removing or rigorously restructuring the DeepSeek component.

### 5. Suggested Reviewer Expertise

Reviewers with the following expertise are recommended: (1) random forest and gradient boosting methods for clinical tabular data, with specific experience in SHAP-based interpretability and calibration evaluation in small-to-moderate cohort settings; (2) composite biomarker construction and mediation analysis methodology, particularly structural equation-adjacent approaches applied to cardiovascular risk prediction; (3) high-altitude physiology and hypoxia-driven organ pathophysiology, ideally with clinical experience in plateau medicine or military medicine settings; (4) clinical prediction modeling standards including TRIPOD-AI reporting and C-statistic generalizability assessment; (5) regulatory and clinical deployment frameworks for AI-powered clinical decision support tools in resource-limited settings.

### 6. State-of-the-Art Literature Review (Past 3 Years)

The HAOI/high-altitude organ injury prediction space is sparse but growing. Chen et al. (*Sci Rep* 2024) developed and validated a machine learning model for myocardial ischemia under high-altitude exposure in PLA soldiers, using a prospective single-institution cohort design. That study is methodologically the most directly comparable antecedent, and the authors cite it (reference 28), yet do not clearly differentiate how TyG-HGB as a feature changes performance relative to the input features Chen et al. employed. In the TyG-ML intersection, Jiang et al. (*Lipids Health Dis*, 2025) developed an explainable ML model incorporating TyG index and ultrasound features for 3-year cardiovascular risk prediction in type 2 diabetes, using SHAP and recursive feature elimination in a 3,358-patient hospitalized cohort. That work demonstrates the same SHAP-TyG pipeline, but in a larger, more heterogeneous population, and must be engaged as a competing methodological reference. Zhang et al. (*BioData Min*, 2024) further showed that ML models predicting TyG-index thresholds achieve AUC 0.873–0.911 in the KNHANES cohort, with performance stratified by sex — a sex-stratification absent from the present manuscript. More broadly, high-altitude risk stratification has progressed toward integrating omics and physiological stress testing: Frontiers in Public Health (2025) published a data-driven identification of metabolic and cardiovascular biomarkers in high-altitude mining workers using RF and SVM on 420,966 examination records, demonstrating the scale at which this question is now being addressed. Against this landscape, the present manuscript represents an incremental single-setting study rather than a field-advancing contribution; the TyG-HGB composite is novel in name, but the functional form is arbitrary, the population is narrower than comparable work, and the SHAP-RF pipeline has been applied in analogous contexts by multiple groups in 2024–2025.

While the methodological approach differs substantially from the present manuscript's biochemical index design, it establishes that altitude illness prediction is advancing toward multi-modal and molecular inputs — a trajectory the present work, grounded entirely in routine laboratory parameters, does not acknowledge.

042660 Outcomes

Section 1: Overall Assessment
This manuscript uses a validated deep learning segmentation model to quantify cardiac chamber and myocardial densities in Hounsfield units (HU) from non-contrast CTAC scans, proposing an elevated myocardium-to-blood-pool density difference as a CT surrogate of anemia independently associated with impaired MFR and incident death or MI. Two large cohorts are used: the REFINE PET registry (n=33,460; 13 sites) and the NLST (n=24,368). The dual-cohort, cross-modality design substantively advances prior small-scale work by Abbasi et al. (Sci Rep, 2022) and Zhou et al. (Eur J Radiol, 2018).
Two concerns dominate. First, the hemoglobin-available subset (n=11,353; 34% of REFINE PET) derives from only two of thirteen sites, and the selection mechanism is inadequately characterised — limiting confidence in the CT-hemoglobin correlations. Second, no incremental predictive value analysis (C-statistic delta, NRI, or IDI) is presented beyond the adjustment models, meaning clinical additive utility remains undemonstrated.

Section 2: Strengths
The 33,460-patient REFINE PET cohort spanning 13 international sites, analysed with a shared frailty Cox model, appropriately handles acquisition heterogeneity and provides adequate outcome depth (median follow-up 1,511 days; 7,790 events).
The automated pipeline — simultaneously segmenting six blood pool regions and LV myocardium from CTAC in under two seconds — shows excellent stress-rest reproducibility (mean bias 0 to −1 HU; 95% LoA −6 to 5 for myocardium–LV), supporting prospective deployment viability.
The NLST validation in a non-cardiac indication population (low-dose lung cancer screening CT) provides genuine cross-modality generalisability, with the myocardium–LV density difference remaining independently associated with cardiovascular mortality (adjusted HR 1.71, 95% CI 1.18–2.47).
The non-linear modelling of the myocardium–LV density difference against MFR (Figure 3) confirms that binary thresholding captures a clinically meaningful inflection and is not an artifact of linear scaling.

Section 3: Weaknesses
The hemoglobin subset's two-site origin introduces uncharacterised selection bias. The Pearson r of 0.18–0.29 between CT density and hemoglobin is substantially lower than the r=0.61–0.78 reported by Abbasi et al. in enriched populations. Without a formal comparison of patients with and without hemoglobin data across all 13 sites, the CT-hemoglobin correlation claim lacks generalisability.
No incremental value analysis is provided. A fully adjusted HR of 1.38 (95% CI 1.26–1.51) is statistically robust but does not establish clinical additive utility without C-statistic or NRI data relative to the existing model including MFR, CAC, LVEF, and TPD. This is the single most critical gap for this readership.
Sex-stratified analyses are absent. The differential HU-to-hemoglobin regression by sex — r=0.81 in men versus r=0.52 in women (Kamel et al., Eur Radiol, 2008) — is well-established, and the 59% male REFINE PET cohort may yield results predominantly driven by the male subgroup.

Section 4: Editorial Decision
Send for Review. The dataset scale, dual-cohort design, and cross-modality validation represent a genuine advance over prior work. Reviewers must adjudicate: (1) whether the two-site hemoglobin subset introduces selection bias sufficient to invalidate the CT-hemoglobin correlation claims; (2) whether the absence of incremental predictive value analysis is fatal to the clinical utility argument; and (3) whether sex-stratified correlation and outcome analyses are required prior to acceptance.

Opportunistic cardiac biomarker extraction from routine CT has advanced rapidly since 2022. Miller et al. (Nat Commun, 2024; Ref. 15 in this manuscript) demonstrated that AI-derived cardiac volumes, LV mass, and coronary calcium from chest CT independently predicted all-cause mortality in the NLST population, with LV mass yielding an adjusted HR of approximately 1.4 per standard deviation. Razipour et al. (J Cardiovasc Comput Tomogr, 2025) extended this to asymptomatic subjects in the EISNER trial, showing LV mass outperformed CAC for cardiovascular death prediction. What the authors should have engaged with more directly is the broader opportunistic CT risk stratification literature (Commandeur et al., Lancet Digital Health, 2020; Zeleznik et al., Lancet Digital Health, 2021), which contextualises their findings within a competitive landscape of body composition, epicardial fat, and coronary calcium biomarkers all competing for the same imaging encounter.

1. Svanström P et al. "Impact of hemoglobin concentration on quantitative cardiac perfusion measurements." Journal of Nuclear Cardiology, 2025 (DOI: 10.1016/j.nuclcard.2025.102537).

1. Overall Assessment
This manuscript addresses a well-motivated problem: the brittleness of single-stream infectious disease forecasting models under data scarcity. The authors assemble a harmonised repository spanning 66 diseases and 13 public sources, then systematically compare single-stream baselines against cross-disease and cross-stream training sets across three model classes — GBM, LSTM, and MOA. The headline claim, that expanded training improves forecasting in 84.9% of cases across models and time series, is empirically grounded and clearly stated. The dataset itself is the manuscript's most distinctive contribution: 101 weekly time series streams, publicly available and documented, representing a scope no prior cross-disease ML forecasting study approaches.
Two concerns dominate. First, the manuscript is submitted in draft form. The author contributions section contains a visible placeholder note. Figures are cited as placeholders throughout the results. The corresponding author (lvandervort@lanl.gov) does not appear in the author list. These are not cosmetic errors; they indicate the manuscript was not reviewed internally before submission. Second, the modelling findings lack novelty against the current literature. The result that multi-source GBM training improves influenza forecasting is the founding premise of Flusion (Ray et al., Epidemics, 2025), which placed first in CDC FluSight 2023/24. Given the structural similarity between Flusion's LightGBM architecture and the GBM component here, the absence of head-to-head comparison on any shared evaluation set is a material gap that cannot be resolved in revision.

2. Strengths
The dataset is the manuscript's most distinctive contribution. The authors compile and harmonise 101 distinct weekly time series streams across 66 infectious diseases from sources including NNDSS, AIDO, Project Tycho, WHO FluNet, and US HHS, with records extending to the 1800s. No prior cross-disease ML forecasting study approaches this scope, and the publicly available repository with documented provenance directly addresses a persistent reproducibility gap.
The experimental design is unusually thorough. Defining four hierarchical training data classes — single stream, single disease, single mode of transmission, all available — and evaluating all three model classes across 20 target streams generating 21,448,672 individual forecasts enables a systematic decomposition of when cross-disease transfer helps and when it induces harm.
The probabilistic evaluation framework matches operational field standards. GBM and LSTM models use quantile regression with pinball loss across seven quantiles; MOA uncertainty is obtained via negative binomial modelling following Murph et al. (PLOS Computational Biology, 2025). Evaluation across MAE, WIS, and 95% prediction interval coverage permits direct external benchmarking against CDC FluSight results.
The negative transfer analysis for dengue is substantive. The authors attribute LSTM and MOA performance degradation under all-vector-borne training to a coefficient-of-variation mismatch between OpenDengue and other vector-borne streams. The GBM's explicit CV features provide a partial mechanistic account for its relative robustness — a level of analytical depth that elevates the paper above a pure benchmarking exercise.

3. Weaknesses
The manuscript is not in a state suitable for peer review. The author contributions section contains an unresolved author note. Figures are referenced as placeholders throughout the results. The corresponding author email does not correspond to any listed author. Nature Communications cannot route this work externally in its current form.
The core modelling results are not benchmarked against the strongest existing baseline. Flusion (Ray et al., Epidemics, 2025) is structurally similar to the GBM component here — LightGBM with quantile regression trained across multiple influenza surveillance signals — and placed first in CDC FluSight 2023/24 among over 30 models. Without head-to-head comparison on shared evaluation data, the claim that expanded training "improves forecasting in the vast majority of cases" is uncontextualised.
All results are aggregated across time series and forecast dates. No disaggregation by geographic location, data quality stratum, or reporting completeness is provided. Given that training data span US domestic and international surveillance with markedly different coverage and population denominators, this aggregation obscures whether transfer gains are broadly distributed or driven by a small subset of high-quality streams.
The dataset contribution, while valuable, is insufficiently characterised as a standalone resource. The manuscript does not document schema, version control, or licensing constraints for commercial ML use. A companion data descriptor in Scientific Data would more appropriately serve this contribution.

4. Editorial Decision
Reject. The manuscript is submitted in incomplete draft form, which alone precludes editorial routing. Beyond the presentation issue, the modelling contribution requires head-to-head benchmarking against Flusion on shared FluSight evaluation sets, and the aggregate evaluation design lacks the geographic and data-quality disaggregation expected at this standard. The authors are encouraged to resubmit after completing the manuscript, adding Flusion comparison, and considering a companion data descriptor in Scientific Data for the repository.

1. Overall Assessment
This manuscript presents a PRISMA-ScR-compliant scoping review of reinforcement learning applied to musculoskeletal motor control, synthesising 92 studies and 172 tasks across five databases through October 2025. The analytical chain spans physics engines, musculoskeletal model characteristics, task taxonomy, reward design, policy representation, learning paradigms, and evaluation practices — supported by a purpose-built bipartite reward-task network analysis. No existing review covers this full chain, and the framing around physics engine evolution, cross-engine portability, and physiological fidelity trade-offs fills a genuine gap relative to Denayer et al. (J. Neuroeng. Rehabil., 2025), whose PRISMA systematic review covers predictive simulations more broadly but does not perform task or reward-level synthesis.
Two concerns dominate. Several quantitative claims in the Discussion — particularly the assertion of PPO dominance and the inverse relationship between model complexity and physiological accuracy — rest on narrative frequency counts without formal synthesis or statistical testing, which is a meaningful gap for a review making prescriptive recommendations. Additionally, the clinical framing in the abstract and introduction is not matched by the synthesis: the 13 practical-application studies in the corpus are never analysed as a distinct subgroup, and the sim-to-real gap is not systematically addressed.

2. Strengths
The five-category task taxonomy is the review's most distinctive contribution. It is operationally defined and applied consistently across all 92 studies and 172 tasks, yielding non-obvious findings via bipartite network analysis: effort regularization emerges as a cross-task regularizer with near-default status (62.6% adoption, k=10 coverage), while imitation is task-specific in ways frequency counts alone would obscure. This level of formal structure is rare in scoping reviews.
The temporal characterisation of physics engine adoption is rigorous and original. The manuscript traces the Simbody-to-MuJoCo transition and the emergence of world-model approaches (four studies, 2023 onwards), linking these to RL's parallelisation requirements and to the NeurIPS Challenge benchmark ecology — content not synthesised anywhere in prior literature.
The five-dimensional data extraction framework is well-structured, piloted prior to formal extraction, and resolved through dual-reviewer consensus. The identification of 65 studies via supplementary means (preprint platforms, expert consultation, backward citation) is appropriate given NeurIPS Challenge outputs' non-standard dissemination. The diagnosis in the Discussion of the "untapped potential of virtual bodies" is scientifically precise: the empirical contribution breakdown (75 methodological optimisation studies vs. 9 knowledge-generation studies) grounds an otherwise qualitative critique in the review's own data.

3. Weaknesses
Evaluation synthesis is under-powered. The authors correctly identify that only 11 studies reported comparative biomechanical analyses and that joint torque and GRF validation each occurred in only three studies, but they do not quantify metric heterogeneity across those 11 studies or analyse the relationship between model complexity and validation quality. Claims about evaluation practice are supported only by frequency counts and visual inspection of Figure 3F.
The clinical framing is not matched by the synthesis. The abstract and introduction invoke rehabilitation and assistive technology, but the 13 practical-application studies are never analysed as a distinct subgroup, the sim-to-real gap is not systematically addressed, and human-exoskeleton interaction receives no discussion of safety constraints, patient-specific parameterisation, or regulatory context. This framing either needs to be tightened or the paper should be explicit that its scope is purely computational.
The treatment of model-based RL is underdeveloped. The four world-model studies (refs 81, 88, 91, 92) are described as a transformative trend, but no structured comparison of their control performance, sample efficiency, or physiological validity against physics-engine baselines is provided. The review relies heavily on NeurIPS MyoChallenge literature as a proxy for field progress without discussing how challenge-driven selection bias shapes the finding that PPO and curriculum learning are dominant paradigms.

4. Editorial Decision
Send for Review. The manuscript's original task taxonomy, bipartite reward analysis, and temporal engine-adoption mapping constitute a structured contribution that goes beyond narrative synthesis. Before peer review, the authors should: (1) tighten or remove the clinical framing to match the actual scope; (2) provide a structured comparison of world-model versus physics-engine RL across the four identified studies; and (3) acknowledge the NeurIPS Challenge selection bias explicitly. Reviewers should adjudicate the methodological rigour of the bipartite network analysis, the completeness of the search relative to Denayer et al. (2025), and whether the first-comprehensive-review claim is defensible.

1. Overall Assessment
This manuscript presents ASE-Reasoner, a two-stage Clinical Longformer-based multitask NLP framework that reclassifies Adult Sepsis Event (ASE)-positive patients as infected or non-infected using discharge summaries, while extracting interpretable diagnostic reasoning spans. The clinical problem is well-motivated: the CDC ASE definition carries a false-positive rate of 42–70%, and no validated NLP adjudication method has previously addressed this task. Internal AUROC is 0.932; external AUROC is 0.929 across MIMIC-IV (US, English) and Severance Hospital (Korea, Korean-to-English translation), with physician-adjudicated labels at both sites.
The principal scientific contribution is the combination of infection classification with clinician-aligned reasoning span extraction in a single deployable pipeline. The two most consequential concerns are: the span extraction Token F1 of 0.348 is never formally related to downstream clinical utility, and the entire external validation rests on LLM-mediated translation introducing an uncharacterised source of systematic error.

2. Strengths
The frozen-encoder design is empirically well-justified. Conventional joint multi-task optimisation collapsed classification AUROC from 0.93 to 0.40–0.63 due to gradient conflict; freezing the Stage 1 encoder before training the span head resolves this completely, a non-trivial architectural finding with broad implications for clinical multi-task NLP.
Cross-institutional validation spanning different healthcare systems, languages, and infection source distributions, with near-identical AUROC (0.932 vs. 0.929), strongly supports discriminative generalisability. Physician adjudication used an identical three-category protocol at both sites, enabling direct comparability of reference standards.
The LLM benchmarking provides a substantively useful negative result. GPT-5.4 mini and Claude Opus 4 generated 11 and 18 false positives respectively on the 100-case external subset versus 2 for ASE-Reasoner at its default threshold, consistent across four model versions and two families.

3. Weaknesses
The Token F1 of 0.348 is low and unsupported by physician correctness evaluation. The Baldwin et al. (AMIA 2018) reference—where F1 of 0.66 was judged clinically correct in 85.3% of cases—operates at a substantially higher level. No analogous assessment is reported here, leaving the interpretability claim clinically unvalidated.
The translation confound in external validation is unquantified. No BLEU score, back-translation analysis, or error rate is reported for Korean-to-English translation via Claude 3.5 Sonnet. If translated notes systematically normalise toward English clinical conventions, the external AUROC may reflect translation artefact rather than true cross-linguistic robustness.
Inter-annotator agreement for the 600-case development cohort adjudication is not reported. Cohen's kappa between the two adjudicating physicians is a critical quality indicator for a study whose validity depends entirely on label integrity.

4. Editorial Decision
Recommended for Send for Review. The frozen-encoder multi-task design and cross-institutional validation constitute a distinctive contribution. Reviewers should adjudicate: (1) whether Token F1 of 0.348 supports clinical utility claims absent a physician correctness evaluation; (2) whether omission of inter-annotator agreement statistics is acceptable; and (3) whether the translation pipeline adequately controls for the Korean-to-English confound in interpreting the external AUROC.
