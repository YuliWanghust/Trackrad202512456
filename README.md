1. Overall Assessment
This manuscript introduces ACLRPRAgent, an LLM-driven decision-support system that generates personalised rehabilitation and return-to-activity prescriptions following ACLR by integrating perioperative EHR curation, dual-metric prognostic prediction of Tegner and ACL-RSI scores, LLM-based attribution analysis, and structured prescription generation. The central claim is that the system outperforms conventional ML and direct-LLM baselines in prognostic accuracy while producing clinically plausible, occupationally tailored rehabilitation outputs. The work addresses a real gap: the translation of dual-domain prognostic assessment into actionable clinician-facing prescriptions — a step beyond score prediction that existing ACLR ML literature has not systematically addressed.
However, the fundamental evidential constraint is that clinical benefit to patients remains undemonstrated. All validity claims rest on retrospective prognostic accuracy within a 115-patient single-health-system cohort and prescription quality judgements by three specialists from the same institution. The absence of independent external validation and the ceiling-effect contamination of the prescription evaluation collectively prevent the authors from supporting the clinical utility claim the framing demands.
2. Strengths
The dual-metric prognostic framework — simultaneous prediction of Tegner score and ACL-RSI — is a genuine conceptual contribution. Existing ACLR ML literature, including Hwang et al. (2024, Digital Health) and a 2025 scoping review in Knee Surgery, Sports Traumatology, Arthroscopy, focuses predominantly on physical performance variables and explicitly flags the absence of psychological readiness prediction as a limitation. Integrating both domains from perioperative EHRs without requiring direct psychological instruments is clinically pragmatic and methodologically novel.
The source-traceable retrieval architecture meaningfully mitigates hallucination risk. By mapping extracted field values back to original curated records rather than generating clinical content, the system addresses the core safety concern that disqualifies most LLM clinical tools at this stage. This design is well-motivated and clearly described.
The expert evaluation methodology — ICC(3,k), exact agreement, and within-one-category agreement across 115 patients and three rubrics spanning 23 evaluation items — is more rigorous than the simple Likert rating paradigms common in LLM clinical output studies. The separation of Tegner and ACL-RSI attribution evaluation into distinct dimensional frameworks adds granularity.
The occupational stratification into four functionally defined cohorts based on activity demand intensity, schedule structure, and daily knee loading provides a principled personalisation mechanism that goes beyond demographic stratification, and is grounded in clinically meaningful distinctions.
3. Weaknesses
The cohort of 115 patients from three campuses of a single health system does not constitute multi-centre generalisation. All data share the same institutional EHR infrastructure, documentation standards, and regional patient demographics. Tegner MAE of 1.18–1.74 and ACL-RSI MAE of 15.3–24.9 cannot be extrapolated to independent institutions with different operative documentation practices, ethnic compositions, or healthcare systems. Without true external validation, generalisability claims are unsupported.
ACL-RSI prediction performance is clinically marginal. An MAE of 15.3–24.9 on a 0–100 scale with clinical threshold at 65 is too imprecise to reliably guide individual-level psychological pacing. The downstream prescription generator uses these predictions as though they carry equivalent informational weight to Tegner predictions, which is unjustified given the acknowledged inferential limitations of predicting psychological readiness from perioperative EHRs capturing surgical rather than behavioural data.
The expert prescription evaluation is uninformative as a discriminative validity measure. Scores concentrated between 4.93–4.99 on a 5-point scale across all nine items; ICC(3,k) for several items approximated zero or were negative. An instrument that cannot discriminate quality across 115 heterogeneous patients provides no useful signal about prescription quality. More critically, the absence of any comparison against clinician-generated standard-of-care prescriptions means it is impossible to establish whether ACLRPRAgent outputs represent improvement, parity, or degradation of current practice.
No ablation studies were conducted. The system integrates retrieval, dual-metric prediction, attribution analysis, and occupational stratification, yet the independent contribution of each component is unquantified. The Qwen3-Max comparator provides partial contextualisation but does not isolate retrieval quality, attribution accuracy, or the value of the occupational framework.
4. Editorial Decision
Reject. The manuscript presents technically careful system design with genuine conceptual novelty in the ACLR rehabilitation space, but the 115-patient single-health-system retrospective cohort, ceiling-effect-contaminated prescription evaluation, absence of external validation, and lack of comparison against clinical standard-of-care collectively preclude the clinical utility claims the authors advance. These deficiencies require substantively new data rather than revision. Resubmission should incorporate external validation at an independent institution, a head-to-head prescription comparison against clinician-generated plans, and formal ablation studies. The work is currently better suited for npj Digital Medicine or Journal of Medical Internet Research.

5. Suggested Reviewer Expertise
Reviewers should cover the following areas: LLM-based clinical agent evaluation with specific familiarity with retrieval-augmented generation architectures and hallucination mitigation in clinical outputs; prognostic modelling for musculoskeletal rehabilitation outcomes, including familiarity with Tegner score prediction and ACL-RSI validation methodology; explainability methods in clinical AI, including attribution analysis design and clinician-facing explanation evaluation; sports medicine and orthopaedic rehabilitation with direct experience in post-ACLR return-to-sport pathway planning and biopsychosocial rehabilitation frameworks; and health AI evaluation methodology including expert rating study design, ceiling effect detection, and inter-rater reliability analysis using ICC.

Against this landscape, ACLRPRAgent's contribution — bridging perioperative EHR-grounded dual-domain prediction to structured, occupationally personalised, clinician-reviewable prescriptions — remains genuinely novel in design scope. No published system in ACLR rehabilitation integrates all four components. However, the field is moving fast: the clinician-directed rehabilitation prescription work and SHAP-validated surgical outcome prediction literature already demonstrate that more rigorous prospective or multi-site evaluation designs are achievable and expected by high-impact reviewers.

1. Overall Assessment
This manuscript evaluates nine predefined cervical cancer screening strategies combining AI-assisted liquid-based cytology (AI-LBC) with HR-HPV testing in a retrospective cohort of 284,002 women screened in Wuhan, China (2020–2022). The central claim is that AI-LBC performs acceptably across standalone, triage, and co-testing configurations, with Strategy 7 (HR-HPV genotyping with reflex AI-LBC) and Strategy 9 (co-testing) offering practical optima. The scale and simultaneous dual-testing design are genuine assets, but the study is geographically confined to a single city using a proprietary algorithm (LANDING LD DNA-ICM II) without external validation, and Bayesian imputation of disease status for unverified participants introduces residual bias that limits generalisability.
2. Strengths
Simultaneous dual-testing of 284,002 women from a single PreservCyt specimen eliminates inter-specimen variability and allows fair head-to-head strategy comparison. Bayesian stratified multiple imputation with prior-sensitivity analyses (Supplementary Tables S6–S9) represents a meaningful methodological advance over typical disease-free assumptions. The bubble chart in Figure 4, encoding sensitivity, referral burden, and Youden's index simultaneously, provides decision-relevant visualisation. Age-stratified analyses across 35–44, 45–54, and 55–64 year cohorts confirm consistent strategy rankings.
3. Weaknesses
External validation is absent; all data derive from one city, one laboratory, and one proprietary frozen algorithm, with technical architecture details deferred to an unavailable Supplementary Note 1. Verification ascertainment relies on inpatient EMR capture within six months, an assumption likely to fail for asymptomatic precancerous lesions managed outpatient. No cost-effectiveness analysis accompanies the strategy recommendations. The nine strategies are threshold-based rule trees with no risk-adaptive component, limiting forward relevance.
4. Editorial Decision
Reject. The single-site, single-algorithm retrospective design without external validation is a structural limitation incompatible with this journal's generalisability standard. The work is competitive at eClinicalMedicine or PLOS Medicine. Resubmission here would require an external validation cohort, formal health economic modelling, and full AI algorithm disclosure.

5. Suggested Reviewer Expertise
Reviewers should include: (1) a specialist in deep learning-based computational pathology for cytological image analysis, with specific familiarity with whole-slide image classification pipelines and frozen model deployment in clinical screening; (2) a biostatistician with expertise in Bayesian multiple imputation for verification bias correction in diagnostic accuracy studies; (3) a cervical cancer screening programme epidemiologist with experience in population-based HPV testing and CIN2+ surveillance methodology, particularly in the Chinese or LMIC context; (4) a health economist with experience in cost-effectiveness modelling of multi-strategy screening programmes; and (5) a gynaecological oncologist or colposcopist with direct experience in organised community cervical screening programme design and implementation.

041206 kidney injury"
Decision: Reject

1. Overall Assessment
This multicentre retrospective study develops a causal ML framework to estimate individualised treatment effects (ITEs) of intraoperative hypotension prevention on postoperative AKI across 141,292 non-cardiac surgical cases from three Korean cohorts. An XGBoost AKI prediction model is trained on INSPIRE, validated externally at KMC and temporally at SNUH, and S-learner counterfactual estimation with IPTW causal grounding is used to derive patient-level ITEs. The motivating argument—that universal blood pressure targets mask substantial treatment-effect heterogeneity—is scientifically sound and clinically timely. However, the methodological execution falls short. The S-learner is theoretically the weakest meta-learner under strong heterogeneity, no alternative estimator is compared, and the threefold variation in high-responder prevalence across cohorts (3.0–8.3%) undermines the claim of a reproducible, deployable phenotype.
2. Strengths
The combined cohort of 141,292 cases across three institutions with distinct time windows provides credible statistical power. The INSPIRE holdout was withheld from all preprocessing and tuning steps, making the reported AUROC of 0.837 a genuine out-of-sample estimate. The benefit-calibration analysis—stratifying patients into predicted benefit quartiles and comparing observed IPTW-adjusted effects within each stratum—is the strongest result in the paper. The preoperative high-responder model (AUROC 0.906–0.873 across cohorts, using only 15 baseline variables) is a clinically meaningful secondary contribution. The decision-curve and cumulative benefit curves provide a well-structured utility assessment, quantifying ITE-based targeting superiority over risk-based selection.
3. Weaknesses
The S-learner, fitted on concatenated treated and untreated data with treatment as a covariate, is known to regularise ITEs toward zero under sparse or continuous exposure—precisely this paper's setting. No T-learner, X-learner, DR-learner, or causal forest comparison is presented. The high-responder prevalence varies threefold across cohorts without mechanistic explanation, suggesting ITE instability under distributional shift. All three cohorts are Korean institutional registries; no demographic subgroup analysis by sex, age decile, or kidney function stage is reported, and no external validation outside East Asia is attempted. The counterfactual scenario—complete elimination of TWA-MBP65—is clinically unachievable, and the resulting ITE estimates are upwardly biased by an unquantified margin.
4. Editorial Decision
Reject. Sole reliance on the S-learner without comparative evaluation against doubly robust alternatives, combined with unexplained high-responder prevalence instability and a clinically unachievable counterfactual, constitutes a fundamental validity gap not addressable by revision. Resubmission to npj Digital Medicine or BJA Open is recommended after redesigning the causal estimation component.

5. Suggested Reviewer Expertise
Reviewers should include specialists in (1) causal inference methodology in observational clinical data, with specific experience in meta-learner selection and validation of ITE estimators under class imbalance and continuous exposure settings; (2) gradient-boosted tree modelling and Platt calibration in high-dimensional clinical tabular data; (3) perioperative haemodynamic physiology and intraoperative blood pressure management in non-cardiac surgery, with familiarity with IPTW-based target-trial emulation; (4) clinical trial design for precision patient selection using pre-randomisation enrichment strategies; and (5) AKI biomarker science and KDIGO outcome classification in surgical cohorts.

6. State-of-the-Art Literature Review (Past 3 Years)
The application of causal ML to perioperative and critical care settings has accelerated considerably. Feuerriegel and colleagues published a comprehensive review of causal ML methods for treatment outcome prediction in Nature Medicine (2024), establishing the methodological reference standard against which this manuscript's choices must be evaluated. That review explicitly identifies S-learner bias toward null effects as a known failure mode under sparse treatment and regularisation, a concern directly applicable here. In the perioperative AKI literature, Saugel et al. (BJA, 2024) demonstrated in 38,338 non-cardiac surgical patients that both intraoperative hypotension and cumulative norepinephrine dose are independently associated with AKI, reinforcing that the intervention itself (vasopressor therapy) carries competing risk—a confound this manuscript does not model. The IMPROVE trial (JAMA, 2025) found that individualised perioperative blood pressure management did not reduce a composite AKI/MINS/mortality endpoint in high-risk abdominal surgery patients, providing the most direct RCT evidence against the manuscript's central premise and a finding the authors should have engaged with explicitly. On causal ML validation, a preprint by Vanderschueren et al. (arXiv 2501.04061, 2025) tested 17 mainstream ITE estimators—including S-, T-, X-, and DR-learners—on two large RCTs and found that none reliably generalised their ITE estimates to held-out data, even without distribution shift. This paper is highly relevant and conspicuously absent from the manuscript's reference list. The present study fills a genuine gap in applying causal ML to haemodynamic management specifically, but must directly address why the S-learner was chosen over doubly robust alternatives and how its ITE estimates behave relative to the generalisation failures documented in Vanderschueren et al.

The authors use doubly robust causal forests rather than an S-learner, providing a methodological benchmark this manuscript should have matched.
Komorowski et al., Nature Medicine, 2023 — Extended the AI Clinician reinforcement learning framework for sepsis management with causal effect estimation, demonstrating that policies targeting predicted high-responder subgroups outperform population-level protocols. Establishes the precedent for benefit-stratified precision targeting in critical care that this paper seeks to replicate in a perioperative setting.

Directly competing observational evidence; uniquely relevant because it models vasopressor exposure as a competing risk, a confound unaddressed in the manuscript under review.
Vanderschueren et al., arXiv 2501.04061, 2025 — Benchmarked 17 causal ML ITE estimators (S-, T-, X-, DR-learners, causal forests) on the International Stroke Trial and Chinese Acute Stroke Trial (N > 40,000 combined), finding universal failure of ITE generalisation to held-out data without distribution shift. The most directly relevant methodological critique of the approach used in this manuscript; its absence from the reference list is a material omission.
Bald et al., npj Digital Medicine, 2024 — Developed and validated a causal ML framework for individualised antihypertensive therapy selection in the general population using large-scale EHR data, demonstrating that high-benefit patient identification using XGBoost-based S-learner ITE estimates substantially reduced NNT compared to risk-based targeting. Directly relevant to interpreting cross-cohort ITE instability and to the preoperative enrichment strategy proposed in the present manuscript.

1. Overall Assessment
This manuscript presents a large-scale retrospective split-plot MRMC study evaluating the HAI-RadX Suite CTC AI Module — a DETR-based hybrid CNN-transformer SaMD — across 47 chest CT findings in 2,322 cases using 49 radiologists. The central claim is that AI-aided reading is non-inferior and largely superior to unaided reading on balanced accuracy across the widest pathological scope yet reported for chest CT, with particular gains in incidental extra-thoracic and time-critical findings. The scale and breadth represent a genuine advance, but two concerns are decisive. First, the majority of authors are employees and shareholders of Harrison.ai, the commercial developer; no independent data access or reanalysis mechanism is described. Second, all 49 readers are Vietnamese radiologists, systematically biasing reader-performance estimates relative to the Australian, American, and French deployment contexts.
2. Strengths
Coverage of 47 findings across pulmonary, vascular, pleural, mediastinal, and extra-thoracic domains exceeds any published chest CT MRMC study. The split-plot design, BH-corrected FDR multiplicity control, and pre-specified non-inferiority margins aligned with FDA SaMD guidance are methodologically sound. The two-plus-one expert adjudication reference standard with proximity-calibrated consensus thresholds is credible. Geographic and scanner diversity across three countries and three manufacturers provides partial ecological validity.
3. Weaknesses
A monocultural Vietnamese reader pool invalidates generalisation of ΔBA estimates to target deployment populations. The test dataset was actively optimised for prevalence and co-occurrence balance, introducing selection bias that inflates apparent external validity. Three findings fail non-inferiority; the manuscript does not address whether these are included in current SaMD regulatory claims or what end-user disclosures are planned. Characterisation accuracy is reported without comparison to clinically calibrated single-finding baselines.
4. Editorial Decision
Send for Review. Reviewers should adjudicate: (1) whether the monocultural reader pool constitutes a fatal validity threat; (2) whether the conflict of interest profile meets the journal's standards for industry-sponsored device evaluations; and (3) whether the three non-inferior findings create regulatory disclosure obligations that must be addressed in the manuscript.

5. Suggested Reviewer Expertise
Reviewers should collectively cover the following domains: (1) MRMC statistical methodology for SaMD reader studies, specifically split-plot designs and bootstrap-based inference under multiplicity; (2) DETR-based and hybrid CNN-transformer architectures for 3D volumetric CT analysis and multi-finding detection pipelines; (3) regulatory science for AI/ML-based SaMDs, with familiarity with FDA 510(k) and De Novo pathways for radiology CADe/CADx devices; (4) thoracic and emergency radiology, with clinical expertise in chest CT interpretation across the breadth of pulmonary, vascular, and musculoskeletal pathologies; and (5) health equity and AI fairness in medical imaging, specifically the generalisability of AI-assisted radiologist performance across culturally and institutionally distinct reader populations.

1. Overall Assessment
This manuscript evaluates four LLMs — DeepSeek-Chat-v3.1, Gemini-2.5-Flash, Grok-4-Fast, and GPT-5-Nano — on simulated pandemic business-closure decision tasks requiring trade-offs between infection risk and economic harm. The central claim is a systematic reasoning–decision dissociation: contextual variables appear in generated justifications but demonstrably fail to influence the underlying choice. The question is policy-relevant and the framing is coherent.
Two structural problems limit the contribution. The scope is confined to a single simplified scenario domain using lightweight conversational model variants, which severely constrains generalizability. The central dissociation finding, while clearly presented, recapitulates what is already documented in the CoT faithfulness literature without advancing on causal grounding. The sweeping conclusions — "current LLMs are not reliable for high-stakes, multi-objective policy decisions" — are not supported by the model sample tested.
2. Strengths
The four prompting strategies increment contextual load progressively, enabling principled attribution of behavioral change. Iterative presentation of all ordered option pairs across 792 tasks under Prompting Strategy 4 mitigates order bias systematically. The combination of Random Forest permutation importance with Elastic-Net logistic regression is appropriate, and cross-validated AUROCs provide defensible model quality evidence. The post-hoc reasoning condition is the most novel element: demonstrating that post-hoc elicitation raises dominated-option selection above 10% for DeepSeek and shifts feature importance away from infection rate toward user preference is a concrete, quantified finding directly relevant to deployed advisory systems.
3. Weaknesses
All tasks are structurally identical binary choices within a single pandemic closure scenario, and all four models are lightweight conversational variants. The paper draws sweeping conclusions that implicitly encompass frontier reasoning models for which no evidence is presented. The dissociation framing is not situated against causal mediation frameworks established by Turpin et al. (2023) and Barez et al. (2025). The temperature of 0.7 with a single fixed seed produces cross-model comparisons without uncertainty quantification, rendering the claim of "substantial variability across models" scientifically underspecified.
4. Editorial Decision
Reject. The manuscript addresses a relevant question with credible analyses, but does not meet bar for Nature Communications or The Lancet Digital Health. Single-domain scope, lightweight-only model coverage, absent seed-variance estimation, and insufficient differentiation from the causal CoT faithfulness literature are collectively disqualifying. Authors should revise with replication across a second policy domain, inclusion of frontier reasoning models, seed-averaged uncertainty quantification, and formal positioning against causal faithfulness methodology before resubmission.

5. Suggested Reviewer Expertise
Reviewers should include expertise in the following areas: (1) LLM behavioral evaluation methodology, specifically prompt sensitivity, sycophancy, and multi-turn decision consistency; (2) causal inference applied to NLP, including mediation analysis and counterfactual probing for reasoning faithfulness; (3) public health policy informatics and computational epidemiology, with familiarity with non-pharmaceutical intervention modeling and pandemic decision frameworks; (4) explainable AI and algorithmic auditing, particularly in high-stakes or policy-adjacent contexts; and (5) behavioral economics or decision science, specifically multi-objective choice under conflicting criteria.

First, the system's core efficiency claim — that iterative evidence-seeking leads to confident diagnoses with fewer tests — is never empirically validated. There is no data on how many tests DxEvolve requests per encounter, nor how that evolves with more DCPs. Second, the choice of base LLMs (Qwen3-30B, DeepSeek-V3.2, GLM-4.7) is unjustified, and no frontier model (Claude Opus, GPT-5-class) is tested — critical for assessing whether DCPs add value beyond raw model capability. Fourth, using the DCP repository to standardise translations of the Chinese PLA cohort is circular — the same repository whose generalisability is being tested is shaping the test data.
Reviewer 2 (Shasha Han) recommends rejection on methodological grounds. The claimed novelty — interactive, self-evolving diagnosis — is not demonstrated. The DCR workflow hardcodes the investigation sequence (physical exam → labs → imaging → guidelines → diagnosis), leaving DCPs little room to influence strategy. DCP gains over guidelines + PubMed alone are ~2.1%, which may be statistical noise. External validation cohorts are severely underpowered (UTI: n=11; liver abscess: n=39). No training, fine-tuning, or reinforcement learning occurs — all prompts are handcrafted. The governance and auditability claims are conceptual, not demonstrated.
Reviewer 3 (Antonis Armoundas) is the most favourable but still recommends major revision. The framework's conceptual framing — moving from static, full-information diagnosis to sequential evidence acquisition — is timely. However, "self-evolution" is more accurately described as supervised, label-aware case-memory. Several process-alignment results are prompt-enforced rather than emergent. The cholecystitis example trace in the supplement raises evidence-leakage concerns. The human comparison overstates performance (n=80, no confidence intervals, no matched clinician-agent comparison). External validation is limited in scale and disease diversity. Statistical reporting is underdeveloped, with p-values but no confidence intervals. Evaluation should include safety, efficiency, and clinical utility metrics, not only top-1 accuracy.

The convergence across reviewers is clear and damaging. The paper's central claims — genuine self-evolution and interactive diagnosis — are not supported by the experimental design. Reviewer 2's rejection recommendation is technically well-grounded and difficult to dismiss. The efficiency gain from DCPs is marginal, the mechanism is supervised case-memory, external validation is statistically inadequate, and no frontier model comparison exists.

0375516 Tiered Hospitals

1. Overall Assessment
This manuscript presents CHD-CARE, a two-stage spatiotemporal transformer framework for four-class pediatric CHD classification (Normal, VSD, ASD, PDA) from color Doppler echocardiography video, with a focus on collaborative human-AI diagnosis across tiered hospital systems. The central scientific contribution is not the classifier itself but the integration of probability-based diagnostic assistance with structured visual guidance — temporal key-frame selection and expert-guided spatial defect localization — evaluated in a randomized three-phase reader study across 14 clinicians at four hospital tiers.
The primary concern is a tension between the scope of the claim and the scope of the evidence. CHD-CARE is evaluated exclusively on isolated, single-subtype acyanotic lesions after excluding concomitant defects and equivocal labels, yet the manuscript frames its contribution as supporting "reliable pediatric CHD diagnosis across tiered hospitals" — a claim that does not hold for the broader CHD phenotype spectrum encountered in practice. The reference standard exclusion of any disagreement between three senior experts artificially removes the hardest cases from evaluation, likely inflating reported performance metrics. These concerns do not invalidate the work but must be resolved before publication.

2. Strengths
The multicenter prospective cohort is a genuine strength. Eight centers spanning national-to-county hospital tiers, with seven fully held-out external validation cohorts encompassing heterogeneous acquisition hardware (Philips EPIQ, GE Vivid, Mindray Resona), support the generalizability claim more credibly than retrospective single-site designs. Pooled external one-vs-rest AUROCs of 0.950–0.984 maintained across this diversity establish a solid foundation for the reader study.
The three-phase crossover reader study is methodologically rigorous within its scope. Latin-square pseudo-randomization and two-week washout periods credibly address recall bias. Stratification by tier and seniority enables granular inference. The use of NRI (0.078, 95% CI 0.066–0.089) and IDI (0.092, 95% CI 0.083–0.101) to quantify decision quality — rather than accuracy alone — is appropriate and uncommon in AI reader studies.
The visual guidance design is technically grounded and honestly calibrated. Rather than applying generic post-hoc attribution (Grad-CAM, Grad-CAM++), the authors use residual deviation from a frozen view-specific normal baseline for key-frame selection, paired with expert-guided spatial attention producing a 44×44 localization box. The blinded acceptability assessment establishing IoU = 0.25 as the reader-accepted threshold is a practical and honest calibration that anchors evaluation to clinical rather than geometric standards.
The reliance calibration analysis adds genuine behavioral insight. Decomposing reader decisions into appropriate reliance, under-reliance, and over-reliance, and demonstrating that visual guidance specifically reduced under-reliance from 13.4% to 7.1%, connects the technical output to a meaningful clinical behavioral claim consistent with the human factors literature on automation trust.

3. Weaknesses
The scope restriction to isolated VSD, ASD, and PDA is understated in the manuscript's framing. Real-world pediatric CHD practice frequently involves concomitant defects and complex phenotypes. The exclusion of concomitant subtypes and equivocal reference labels from the analytic cohort concentrates evaluation on the diagnostic scenarios where AI assistance is least needed. The authors should quantify the concomitant-exclusion rate from their screened 4,968 subjects and bound the clinical claim accordingly.
The consensus reference standard introduces structural optimism. For cases without procedural confirmation — including normal examinations, mild lesions, and spontaneously closed defects — any expert disagreement among three senior echocardiographers led to exclusion. This removes precisely the ambiguous cases where clinician error is highest and AI support most clinically relevant, inflating reported sensitivity, specificity, and AUROC.
The reader study has two underappreciated confounds. First, 14 readers is an insufficient sample for stable subgroup inference across eight strata (four tiers × two seniority levels), and confidence intervals on subgroup metrics are largely absent; individual-reader-level claims such as "provincial junior readers improved from 55.4% to 96.3%" are not statistically robust. Second, the study was conducted with known case prevalence (120 positive, 120 normal) under controlled retrospective conditions, which does not reflect real-world screening workflows with variable prevalence, incomplete examinations, and time pressure.
Prevalence-adjusted calibration is absent. The cohort was assembled with near-balanced positive-to-negative ratios that differ substantially from outpatient screening prevalence. Calibration curves and expected calibration error are not reported, making the probability outputs clinically uninterpretable for deployment settings.

4. Editorial Decision
Send for External Review, conditional on major revision. The prospective multicenter cohort, three-phase reader study, and reliance calibration analysis together represent evaluation rigor sufficient for substantive peer review. No single flaw is unresolvable, but reviewers must adjudicate: (1) whether the clinical scope claim is appropriately bounded relative to isolated acyanotic lesion performance; (2) whether the consensus-exclusion reference standard inflates the primary metrics in a way that materially affects the conclusions; and (3) whether prevalence-adjusted calibration analysis is feasible and should be required as a condition of acceptance.

5. Suggested Reviewer Expertise
Reviewers should cover: spatiotemporal video transformer architectures and attention-based interpretability for medical video classification; multi-view fusion and incomplete-data robustness in echocardiographic deep learning; randomized crossover human-AI interaction study design with specific attention to reliance calibration, NRI/IDI methodology, and crossover confound analysis; pediatric echocardiography and CHD diagnosis in tiered or resource-limited healthcare settings with experience spanning acyanotic and complex congenital phenotypes; and clinical AI evaluation methodology with emphasis on probabilistic calibration, prevalence adjustment, and real-world deployment readiness per DECIDE-AI and TRIPOD+AI standards.

The echocardiography AI field has advanced substantially toward foundation-scale and collaborative frameworks. Christensen et al. (Nat. Med. 2024) developed EchoCLIP, a vision-language foundation model trained on echocardiogram-report pairs across a broad range of cardiac conditions. Vukadinovic et al. (Nature 2025/2026) subsequently published EchoPrime, a multi-video view-informed model trained on 12 million video-report pairs delivering comprehensive study-level predictions. Both represent the frontier of general echocardiographic representation learning against which CHD-CARE's ResNet-50-plus-transformer architecture should be benchmarked, but the manuscript does not engage with either. In the pediatric CHD domain, Cheng et al. (Research 2024) developed a multi-view multi-modal deep learning framework integrating 2D and Doppler TTE from Beijing Children's Hospital for VSD/ASD detection, achieving mean AUC of 0.996 internally — a direct methodological comparator that the manuscript cites but does not benchmark against. Lukyanenko et al. (Circulation 2026) developed EchoFocus-CHD, building on PanEcho's view-agnostic architecture with a transformer encoder to detect 12 critical and 8 non-critical CHD lesions, validated externally across echos from 58 countries across 6 continents — a scope that directly challenges CHD-CARE's generalizability framing. Day et al. (Ultrasound Obstet Gynecol 2024) directly studied clinician-AI collaboration for fetal atrioventricular septal defect detection and found that providing grad-CAM overlays did not improve and in some conditions worsened collaborative performance when the AI was incorrect — a finding directly contradicting CHD-CARE's visual-guidance benefit claims that demands engagement. The manuscript does not engage with either the Day et al. or PROTEUS findings, both of which represent the most directly relevant human-AI collaboration evidence and raise the question of whether visual guidance benefits generalize beyond controlled reader studies.
