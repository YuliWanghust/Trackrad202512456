036144 Sensing

1. Overall Assessment
This manuscript presents PDSensing, an actor-critic reinforcement learning framework that sequentially recommends smartphone test activities for PD detection, claiming comparable AUROC to full-data baselines using only 37.5–50% of test records. The fusion of CNN embeddings across walking, tapping, and voice modalities is technically coherent, and the dual-reward Critic design is well-motivated.
The "first study" claim requires qualification — Bernardino et al. (MICCAI 2022) and Norcliffe et al. (2024) addressed structurally identical sequential modality selection problems in clinical settings. More critically, validation rests entirely on mPower, a dataset with self-reported PD diagnosis, which fundamentally limits the clinical credibility of the performance claims.

2. Strengths
The dual-reward mechanism is the paper's most distinctive technical contribution. The ablation in Table 2 confirms that individual-level reward alone improves over no-reward PDSensing, and that adding population-level pairwise ranking further gains AUROC, reaching 0.89 at 10 records versus 0.82 without reward. This incremental validation is well-structured.
Table 3 demonstrates that multimodality consistently outperforms all single-modality configurations, particularly at low record counts (≤4), supporting the fusion design with empirical evidence rather than assumption.
The iPhone deployment benchmarks (Table 4: <50% CPU typical, 48.5 MB memory, iPhone 8 compatible) and the IRB-approved OSUWMC usability study distinguish this from a purely algorithmic contribution.

3. Weaknesses
Validation is critically insufficient. All experiments use mPower with self-reported diagnosis as ground truth. Cohort size, class balance, and demographic distribution are not reported in the main text. No external cohort with clinically confirmed PD diagnosis is presented. AUROC of 0.89 on self-labelled data does not constitute diagnostic evidence.
The hyperparameters γ and α^y, which govern reward weighting and class rebalancing respectively, have no sensitivity analysis. Given likely class imbalance in mPower, α^y materially affects which activities are recommended — this is not a minor gap.
Usability claims are unsubstantiated. The deployment section asserts "effective Human-AI interaction" without reporting participant counts, SUS scores, or task completion rates from the OSUWMC study.

4. Editorial Decision
Desk Reject. The absence of external validation on a clinically annotated cohort is presently unresolvable at review, and the usability claims are unsupported by reported data. The work is better suited to npj Digital Medicine or JMIR mHealth and uHealth. Resubmission may be considered if an independent cohort with confirmed clinical diagnosis is added.

5. Suggested Reviewer Expertise
Reviewers should span the following areas: actor-critic reinforcement learning for sequential feature acquisition in partially observed settings; multimodal sensor fusion using CNN-based embeddings for wearable or smartphone data; clinical validation methodology for PD digital biomarkers, specifically mPower and PPMI dataset experience; neurologist with PD diagnosis expertise and familiarity with UPDRS-based assessment as a reference standard; and mobile health app deployment methodology including IRB design for remote participant studies and regulatory considerations for AI-based diagnostic tools.

6. State-of-the-Art Literature Review (Past 3 Years)
Smartphone-based PD detection has advanced substantially on the PhoneMD (Schwab & Karlen, AAAI 2019) and PDTap (Deng et al., 2022) foundations that this paper uses as baselines. Atri et al. (2022) demonstrated deep learning for daily out-of-clinic monitoring using wearable sensors on the MJFF-funded mPower cohort, achieving strong discrimination with passive sensing — a passive paradigm this paper must contend with more directly. Zhu et al. (2025, PLOS ONE) showed a hybrid 1D-CNN/BiGRU model on spiral drawing tasks achieving competitive detection with 58 participants, illustrating that task-specific active protocols remain competitive even in small cohorts. In the active sensing domain, the IBFA work (Norcliffe et al., 2024) and the stochastic encoders for active feature acquisition (SEFA, 2025) represent the current frontier of non-RL and RL-based acquisition policies. Bernardino et al. (MICCAI 2022) demonstrated RL-based sequential modality selection for cardiac diagnosis on a structurally equivalent formulation. The PDSensing manuscript does not engage meaningfully with this active sensing literature and does not position its dual-reward mechanism against these alternatives. This gap weakens the paper's claim to novelty and must be addressed in any revision.

036233 retinal diseases

1. Overall Assessment
This manuscript presents InfantAngio, a framework for synthesizing FFA from CFP in infantile retinal disease, reformulating the generation process as a deterministic Probability Flow ODE transport trajectory conditioned by a RETFound-based Image Prompt Adapter. The dataset scale — 83,950 image pairs from 259 infants — and the downstream diagnostic utility analysis represent genuine effort.
The central problem is that the paper conflates synthesis quality with clinical safety. A Cohen's Kappa of −0.005 in the Visual Turing Test is interpreted as visual indistinguishability rather than expert unreliability on a poorly powered task. The AUC improvement from 0.984 (CFP-only) to 0.993 (CFP + synthetic FFA) is statistically significant but clinically marginal, and overlapping confidence intervals with the real-FFA upper bound (0.994) are used to argue clinical equivalence — a logical error.

2. Strengths
The dataset construction is the manuscript's most defensible contribution. 83,950 CFP-FFA pairs from a pediatric population spanning ROP, Coats' disease, and retinoblastoma, with patient-level train-evaluation stratification across 209/50 infants, addresses a genuine scarcity and reflects appropriate leakage awareness.
The deterministic latent transport trajectory is technically coherent. The scaled linear power beta schedule enforcing a zero-terminal SNR boundary condition, combined with velocity prediction parameterisation, is supported by the ablation in Table 3, which attributes FID reduction from 72.76 to 64.26 and LPIPS improvement from 0.394 to 0.388 to this component specifically.
The RETFound Perceiver-based Resampler compressing ophthalmic embeddings into 16 latent queries further reduces FID to 62.20 and improves Recall to 0.929, with component-level attribution that many synthesis papers omit.

3. Weaknesses
All 259 infants originate from a single centre. The MPOS generalisation experiment retrains the model entirely on an adult distribution — it is not external validation of the infantile model. No multi-site or prospective cohort is presented. For a tool framed as a population-level screening alternative to invasive angiography, this is disqualifying at this tier.
The downstream diagnostic evaluation has an unaddressed circularity: RETFound serves as both the Image Prompt Adapter backbone during synthesis and the feature extractor for the downstream classifier. Any measured AUC benefit may partly reflect shared representational biases rather than the informational content of synthetic FFA. No ablation with an independent classifier is provided.
The Visual Turing Test is underpowered. Four graders evaluate approximately 10 images per disease category. The reported Chi-square P-value of 1.000 is anomalous and warrants scrutiny. Critically, the test assesses visual plausibility, not the primary safety question: whether synthetic images introduce false pathological features that could alter clinical management.

4. Editorial Decision
Reject. Single-centre provenance, absence of external validation for the core infantile model, architectural circularity in the diagnostic benchmark, and an underpowered Turing Test collectively constitute fundamental gaps incompatible with the paper's screening-replacement clinical framing. The work has technical merit and would be competitive at MICCAI, Medical Image Analysis, or IEEE JBHI.

5. Suggested Reviewer Expertise
Reviewers should include: (1) expertise in probabilistic flow matching and ODE-based generative models for cross-modality medical image synthesis, specifically in weakly-paired or unregistered settings; (2) expertise in latent diffusion architectures and foundation model adaptation, including IP-Adapter and Perceiver-based conditioning mechanisms; (3) expertise in paediatric retinal imaging, specifically ROP screening workflows and the clinical use of FFA in neonatal ophthalmology; (4) expertise in evaluation methodology for generative medical imaging, including Turing test design, distribution-level metrics, and hallucination quantification; (5) expertise in regulatory science and clinical AI validation, particularly FDA/CE requirements for diagnostic imaging software intended for paediatric populations.

6. State-of-the-Art Literature Review
The CFP-to-FFA synthesis problem is well-established and increasingly competitive. Wang et al. (2024, MCHM '24) introduced the MPOS benchmark — the same dataset used in this paper's generalisation experiment — alongside an LDM-based approach for multi-disease paired synthesis, setting a direct and largely unacknowledged competitive baseline. The LPUWF-LDM (Expert Systems with Applications, 2025) and the Spatial-frequency dual-constrained Mamba diffusion model (Medical Image Analysis, 2025) demonstrate that the field has moved toward Mamba-based state-space architectures and frequency-domain constraints that address structural fidelity without deterministic trajectory reformulation. UWAFA-GAN (IEEE JBHI, 2024) established registration-aware synthesis at scale and remains a harder baseline than the authors acknowledge. In the ROP screening domain, Wang et al. (JAMA Network Open, 2021) demonstrated AUCs of 0.983–0.998 for automated ROP classification from CFP alone on cohorts substantially larger than this paper's evaluation set — which raises the unaddressed question of whether synthetic FFA adds clinically meaningful information that well-trained CFP classifiers cannot already capture. The authors cite several of these works but do not engage with their specific methodological advances or the implications of the CFP-only classification ceiling for their clinical thesis.

036204 Chinese medicine

1. Overall Assessment
This manuscript evaluates 16 LLMs across seven prompt strategies on Taiwan's 2025 national TCM licensing examination (475 questions), arguing that model selection dominates prompt engineering as the primary performance determinant, and that prompt–model interactions are strongly model-dependent. The study extends prior single-model work by the same group and addresses a genuinely underexplored question in the TCM AI literature.
Two concerns are critical. The use of a single national examination from one jurisdiction as the sole benchmark limits generalisability of both performance estimates and error taxonomy claims. The conclusions are also largely confirmatory — model-family dominance over prompt effects has been observed in Western medical benchmarks — and the paper does not adequately explain the mechanistic basis for the observed interaction patterns.

2. Strengths
Testing 16 architecturally diverse models under identical inference settings with seven explicitly defined, systematically ablated prompt variants substantially reduces confounding typical of single-model studies. The logistic regression framework — with fixed effects for model family, prompt variant, domain, cognitive level, vignette presence, and question polarity, plus pre-specified model-by-prompt interaction terms — is appropriately specified for the research question.
The three-tier error taxonomy applied to CoT-generated explanations, validated by three-model consensus and calibrated against two TCM-specialist human raters (inter-rater agreement 89.0%, human-consensus agreement 74.5%), is the most distinctive methodological contribution. The characterisation of o3's answer–explanation discordance (κ = 0.656, 16.6% wrong answers with valid explanations) versus DeepSeek-V3.2 (κ = 0.907, 0.6%) identifies architecturally distinct failure modes with real clinical deployment implications.

3. Weaknesses
The benchmark is a single closed-ended multiple-choice examination from one administration year in one jurisdiction. Concurrent TCM benchmarking efforts — MTCMB, TCM-5CEval, TCM-Eval — include classical literacy, clinical decision-making, and non-pharmacological therapy tasks that would test whether the misunderstanding-dominant error profile holds across formats. The authors do not engage with this literature.
GPT-4 as the regression reference is justified by continuity with prior work but is structurally problematic: its consistently low baseline inflates comparator odds ratios (Claude aOR = 7.48), making effect sizes partially an artefact of reference selection. A sensitivity analysis using a mid-performing model such as GPT-4o as reference is necessary.
Claude-opus-4.5, GPT-5.2, and Grok-4 had training cutoffs overlapping the July 2025 examination date. The argument that answer-level contamination is unlikely because official answers were released post-cutoff is insufficient: question-level exposure during pre-training could inflate performance through item familiarity. No contamination detection analysis is reported.

4. Editorial Decision
Transfer to a specialist journal (npj Digital Medicine or Artificial Intelligence in Medicine) is recommended. The manuscript is methodologically sound but its contribution is empirical rather than conceptual, and its readership fit at Nature Communications is limited. The unresolved contamination concern for three models must be addressed before publication anywhere. Reviewers should adjudicate: (1) whether the single-examination design supports the generalisability claims; (2) whether the GPT-4 reference category distorts reported model-family effect sizes; and (3) whether the error taxonomy findings are valid given restriction to lower-performing models under CoT-only conditions.

5. Suggested Reviewer Expertise
Reviewers should include expertise in large-scale LLM evaluation methodology with experience in benchmark design and contamination detection for autoregressive language models; logistic regression and mixed-effects modelling for clustered educational outcome data; TCM clinical practice at the level of licensing examination design or clinical curriculum development in Taiwan or Mainland China; and prompt engineering for domain-specific reasoning tasks, particularly chain-of-thought elicitation in non-English language settings.

6. State-of-the-Art Literature Review
The TCM LLM evaluation landscape has expanded considerably since 2023. MTCMB (2025) introduced a multi-task benchmark spanning knowledge recall, reasoning, and safety dimensions specifically for TCM. TCM-5CEval extended earlier TCM-3CEval work to five dimensions including classical literacy and clinical non-pharmacological therapy, evaluating 15 models and identifying permutation-based inference fragility as a systematic weakness. TCM-Eval offers a dynamic, extensible benchmark with expert-validated items across TCM fundamentals, clinical practice, integrated Western medicine, and medical humanities. A scoping review of 27 tuning and evaluation studies in TCM (through May 2025) confirmed that knowledge-based errors predominate in lower-performing models and that fine-tuning smaller architectures yields limited gains against frontier general-purpose models at scale — directly corroborating the present manuscript's conclusions, though the authors do not cite this convergent evidence.
Against this landscape, the present manuscript's contribution is the systematic prompt ablation design applied across a large model panel, which none of the above benchmarks implement at comparable scale. The three-tier error taxonomy is also a differentiator. However, the manuscript's engagement with this concurrent literature is thin: MTCMB, TCM-5CEval, and TCM-Eval are not cited or discussed, which means the authors have not positioned their single-examination findings against multi-dimensional benchmarks that could either validate or challenge the generalisability of their error taxonomy. This is a substantive gap that must be addressed in revision.

036666 Statistical Analysis

1. Overall Assessment
This manuscript proposes CorePMNet, integrating anatomical prior-guided segmentation with a handcrafted statistical scoring module (CL-SASM) for cruciate ligament MRI analysis on a single-institution dataset (MCLHH, n=68). The core claim is that a population-derived probability-map prior improves segmentation convergence and boundary accuracy, while interpretable morphological scoring replaces opaque classifiers.
The conceptual framing is coherent and the interpretability motivation is clinically legitimate. However, the evaluation rests entirely on 68 patients from one hospital, with no external validation, no statistically grounded confidence intervals, and a circular dependency between the segmentation training set and the CL-SASM normal reference distribution. The 100% sensitivity claim on 16 tear cases is a point estimate from a sample too small to support the clinical conclusions drawn.

2. Strengths
The CorePK construction pipeline is technically well-specified. Voxel-wise averaging over resampled masks followed by signed distance transform and sigmoid mapping yields a continuous, differentiable 3D prior. The stochastic prior modulation module, gating prior influence via a learnable coefficient and uniform random scaling (α ~ U(0.8, 1.2)), directly addresses known over-reliance failure modes in prior-injection architectures.
CL-SASM derives classification scores directly from interpretable morphological features — skeletal continuity ratio, thickness statistics, intensity standard deviation, boundary gradient — rather than post-hoc attribution. This chain-of-evidence design aligns with how musculoskeletal radiologists reason and is the paper's most clinically distinctive contribution.
The correlation analysis in Section 5.3 showing that skel_major_ratio dominates discrimination while glcm_contrast carries near-zero variance provides principled feature selection beyond ablation, supporting the interpretability claim.

3. Weaknesses
The dataset is critically small and single-source. Segmentation performance is evaluated on five patients from MCLHH-A; the 31.1% HD95 reduction claim over DeepLabV3+ rests on five observations with no confidence intervals and no cross-validation. Scanner and field-strength differences between subsets (GE 1.5T vs. Philips 3T) introduce uncontrolled systematic confounds. No evaluation on public datasets such as MRNet or OAI is provided.
The CL-SASM normal reference is constructed from MCLHH-A, the same subset used for segmentation training and evaluation. This circular dependency means scoring inherits upstream biases. The 100% sensitivity on 16 tears carries a Wilson confidence interval of approximately 79–100%, unreported. No radiologist benchmark is provided — a paper claiming clinical auxiliary utility must compare against reported radiologist sensitivity and specificity for CL tear, approximately 86% and 95% respectively in published meta-analyses.
The CL-SASM weight coefficients (α₁–α₄, β₁–β₅, γ in Equations 15–18) are never defined or motivated, yet they fully determine classification behavior. No ablation or sensitivity analysis over these weights is reported. For a module whose entire clinical claim rests on interpretability and reproducibility, this omission is disqualifying.

4. Editorial Decision
Reject. The single-institution dataset of 68 patients cannot support the clinical claims made, CL-SASM weight coefficients are undefined, the circular use of MCLHH-A compromises the diagnostic evaluation, and no radiologist comparison is provided. With external validation, formal statistical treatment of classification metrics, and explicit coefficient reporting, the work could be appropriate for Medical Image Analysis or Computerized Medical Imaging and Graphics.

5. Suggested Reviewer Expertise
Reviewers should include specialists in: (1) anatomical shape prior modeling and signed-distance-based constraint injection in U-Net architectures for 3D medical image segmentation; (2) musculoskeletal MRI, specifically knee ligament assessment and ACL/PCL injury grading in clinical radiology; (3) statistical methods for small-sample clinical AI evaluation, including confidence interval estimation, calibration, and bootstrap validation; (4) interpretable machine learning for medical decision support, with experience distinguishing post-hoc attribution from inherently interpretable model architectures; and (5) multimodal MRI fusion in orthopaedic or sports medicine imaging.

6. State-of-the-Art Literature Review
The field of deep learning for ACL and knee ligament analysis has matured considerably. A 2024 multicenter study by Wang et al. (Quantitative Imaging in Medicine and Surgery, 2024) demonstrated ACL injury detection with multicenter validation, and a 2024 review in Arthroscopy reported that deep learning models can enhance clinician diagnostic accuracy beyond 96% for ACL ruptures. DCLU-Net (2022) combined semi-supervised U-Net segmentation with radiomic features, achieving Dice scores of 0.84 for intact ACL and 0.73 for fully ruptured ACL, directly competing with the segmentation claims here. In parallel, the anatomical prior literature has advanced: AIC-Net (AAAI 2025) introduced learnable, patient-specific differentiable anatomical priors for multi-organ segmentation, and the nnU-Net framework with organ-specific prior schemes (2025) demonstrated systematic gains on difficult small-structure tasks. Knowledge-guided SAM variants with CRF-based anatomical constraints have also emerged (KG-SAM, 2025). Against this backdrop, the CorePMNet prior mechanism — a static population-averaged SDT with stochastic scaling — is conceptually straightforward and does not engage with learnable or deformable prior paradigms that are now standard. The CL-SASM interpretability contribution is more distinctive relative to this literature, but the absence of any comparison with radiomics pipelines such as PyRadiomics-based feature extraction or hybrid radiomic-deep learning approaches weakens the novelty claim.

036756 Classification" (MOSAIC)

1. Overall Assessment
This manuscript presents MOSAIC, a five-stage pipeline combining a mask-aware multimodal transformer, sensor-availability adversarial alignment, sharpness-aware minimization, and teacher-guided label expansion for zero-shot ICU false-alarm classification. The central claim is that cross-cohort failure arises from three identifiable forms of internal over-adaptation: architectural zero-imputation conflating absent and degraded channels, initialization-biased teacher-guided expansion, and internal AUROC as a poor proxy for external transfer. On CinC 2015 and MIMIC-II, MOSAIC achieves AUROCs of 0.786 and 0.769, substantially exceeding supervised and adversarial comparators.
The reframing of cross-cohort failure as a design-level generalization problem is conceptually meaningful. However, the labeled development cohort of 1,077 records from 45 patients at a single Korean center using a single Philips IntelliVue monitor undermines the generalizability claims. Combined with critically low external specificity and an absence of literature-level baselines, this manuscript does not meet the bar for Nature Communications.

2. Strengths
The structural masking of absent tokens from self-attention — distinct from both zero-imputation and presence-flagging — is precisely motivated. Table 3 shows mean external AUROC drops from 0.7775 to 0.6433 on removing this mechanism, and the flat-versus-drop behavioral audit provides mechanistic clarity that the zero-imputation variants collapse both conditions into the same degenerate decision pattern.
The evaluation protocol is strict: CinC 2015 and MIMIC-II were withheld entirely from development, checkpoint selection, and threshold calibration. Modality-removal stress tests and flat-line corruption analyses (Table 2) reinforce robustness claims beyond a single clean-metric evaluation.
The initialization-teacher 2×2 factorial (Table 5) isolates a non-obvious finding — initialization geometry dominates teacher substitution in determining external transfer, with replacing the teacher under fixed SA-Adv initialization actually reducing external AUROC from 0.6129 to 0.5777 — that has direct implications for teacher-student frameworks broadly.

3. Weaknesses
The labeled cohort is critically inadequate: 1,077 records from 45 patients at one institution, yielding 21 training patients for supervised stages. Category-level analyses show markedly worse performance for ASYSTOLE and V-TACH — the alarm types with the highest clinical consequence — yet the manuscript does not quantify the patient safety implications of this failure pattern. This is a potential harm signal, not merely a limitation.
External specificity at the fixed threshold of 0.5 is 0.392 on MIMIC-II, meaning over 60% of true alarms would be suppressed. A false-alarm suppression system that misses the majority of true alarms poses direct patient risk. The authors do not address operating point selection in a safety context, and no clinical harm analysis is provided.
The comparator set contains only pipeline variants. No published deep learning baseline from the literature — contrastive learning approaches on CinC 2015, diffusion-based classifiers, or rule-ensemble methods that dominated the original challenge — is included. The claim of advancing the field cannot be assessed without these comparisons.

4. Editorial Decision
Reject. The manuscript has fundamental, unresolvable flaws at this submission stage. The 45-patient single-center labeled cohort is insufficient to support the scope of claims. External specificity of 0.392 on MIMIC-II represents a patient safety liability that precludes publication without prospective clinical risk analysis. The absence of literature-level baselines makes the performance claims unverifiable against the state of the art. The authors are encouraged to substantially expand the labeled development cohort through multicenter collaboration, conduct a clinical operating-point analysis with explicit harm quantification, and include published external baselines before resubmission. This work may be appropriate for transfer to npj Digital Medicine or PLOS Digital Health upon addressing the cohort and safety issues, where the methodological contribution on structural absence masking and initialization geometry could be published at appropriate scope.

5. Suggested Reviewer Expertise
Reviewers should include specialists in: transformer-based multimodal sequence modeling with missing modalities (specifically key-padding masking and attention under partial observability); domain adaptation and distribution shift for time-series physiological signals, including adversarial and self-supervised methods; ICU bedside monitoring, alarm fatigue, and clinical decision support in critical care; loss-landscape geometry analysis and sharpness-aware minimization in deep learning; and biomedical signal processing with experience in ECG/ABP/PPG artifact characterization across heterogeneous acquisition environments.

6. State-of-the-Art Literature Review (Past 3 Years)
The ICU false-alarm literature has moved incrementally over the past three years. Kiyasseh et al. (Scientific Reports, 2022) applied contrastive learning to CinC 2015, demonstrating improved representation learning over CNN baselines but still within a single-dataset evaluation paradigm. A diffusion model with contrastive learning (IJCAI 2023) extended generative approaches to arrhythmia alarm classification but likewise evaluated on CinC 2015 without cross-site validation. A recent VTaC-based CNN-attention approach (arXiv 2025) reported AUROCs exceeding 0.96 on ventricular tachycardia in single-site conditions. The absence of zero-shot cross-cohort evaluation in the dominant literature is precisely the gap this manuscript targets. In the broader waveform domain generalization space, MERL-ECHO (medRxiv 2025) demonstrated CLIP-based zero-shot ECG-to-echocardiogram transfer at multi-center scale, and CLIMB (arXiv 2025) provided evidence for multitask pretraining improving cross-domain generalization in clinical multimodal models. MOSAIC's framing — that initialization geometry, not teacher quality, drives transfer — has not been examined in any of these works and constitutes a potentially distinguishing contribution. However, the manuscript does not engage with the broader domain adaptation literature including transformer-based test-time adaptation or source-free domain adaptation methods, which have proposed solutions to the exact selection problem identified here (internal AUROC as a poor external proxy). That gap should be addressed.
