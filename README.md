Editorial Report: CHD-CARE — Collaborative AI for Reliable Pediatric Congenital Heart Disease Diagnosis Across Tiered Hospitals

1. Overall Assessment
This manuscript presents CHD-CARE, a two-stage spatiotemporal transformer framework for four-class pediatric CHD classification (Normal, VSD, ASD, PDA) from color Doppler echocardiography video, with a focus on collaborative human-AI diagnosis across tiered hospital systems. The central scientific contribution is not the classifier itself but the integration of probability-based diagnostic assistance with structured visual guidance — temporal key-frame selection and expert-guided spatial defect localization — evaluated in a randomized three-phase reader study across 14 clinicians at four hospital tiers.
The primary concern is a tension between the scope of the claim and the scope of the evidence. CHD-CARE is evaluated exclusively on isolated, single-subtype acyanotic lesions after excluding concomitant defects and equivocal labels, yet the manuscript frames its contribution as supporting "reliable pediatric CHD diagnosis across tiered hospitals" — a claim that does not hold for the broader CHD phenotype spectrum encountered in practice. The reference standard exclusion of any disagreement between three senior experts artificially removes the hardest cases from evaluation, likely inflating reported performance metrics. These concerns do not invalidate the work but must be resolved before publication.

2. Strengths
The multicenter prospective cohort is a genuine strength. Eight centers spanning national-to-county hospital tiers, with seven fully held-out external validation cohorts encompassing heterogeneous acquisition hardware (Philips EPIQ, GE Vivid, Mindray Resona), support the generalizability claim more credibly than retrospective single-site designs. Pooled external one-vs-rest AUROCs of 0.950–0.984 maintained across this diversity establish a solid foundation for the reader study.
The three-phase crossover reader study is methodologically rigorous within its scope. Latin-square pseudo-randomization and two-week washout periods credibly address recall bias. Stratification by tier and seniority enables granular inference. The use of NRI (0.078, 95% CI 0.066–0.089) and IDI (0.092, 95% CI 0.083–0.101) to quantify decision quality — rather than accuracy alone — is appropriate and uncommon in AI reader studies.
The visual guidance design is technically grounded and honestly calibrated. Rather than applying generic post-hoc attribution (Grad-CAM, Grad-CAM++), the authors use residual deviation from a frozen view-specific normal baseline for key-frame selection, paired with expert-guided spatial attention producing a 44×44 localization box. The blinded acceptability assessment establishing IoU = 0.25 as the reader-accepted threshold is a practical and honest calibration that anchors evaluation to clinical rather than geometric standards.
The reliance calibration analysis adds genuine behavioral insight. Decomposing reader decisions into appropriate reliance, under-reliance, and over-reliance, and demonstrating that visual guidance specifically reduced under-reliance from 13.4% to 7.1%, connects the technical output to a meaningful clinical behavioral claim consistent with the human factors literature on automation trust.

3. Weaknesses
The scope restriction to isolated VSD, ASD, and PDA is understated in the manuscript's framing. Real-world pediatric CHD practice frequently involves concomitant defects and complex phenotypes. The exclusion of concomitant subtypes and equivocal reference labels from the analytic cohort concentrates evaluation on the diagnostic scenarios where AI assistance is least needed. The authors should quantify the concomitant-exclusion rate from their screened 4,968 subjects and bound the clinical claim accordingly.
The consensus reference standard introduces structural optimism. For cases without procedural confirmation — including normal examinations, mild lesions, and spontaneously closed defects — any expert disagreement among three senior echocardiographers led to exclusion. This removes precisely the ambiguous cases where clinician error is highest and AI support most clinically relevant, inflating reported sensitivity, specificity, and AUROC.
The reader study has two underappreciated confounds. First, 14 readers is an insufficient sample for stable subgroup inference across eight strata (four tiers × two seniority levels), and confidence intervals on subgroup metrics are largely absent; individual-reader-level claims such as "provincial junior readers improved from 55.4% to 96.3%" are not statistically robust. Second, the study was conducted with known case prevalence (120 positive, 120 normal) under controlled retrospective conditions, which does not reflect real-world screening workflows with variable prevalence, incomplete examinations, and time pressure.
Prevalence-adjusted calibration is absent. The cohort was assembled with near-balanced positive-to-negative ratios that differ substantially from outpatient screening prevalence. Calibration curves and expected calibration error are not reported, making the probability outputs clinically uninterpretable for deployment settings.

4. Editorial Decision
Send for External Review, conditional on major revision. The prospective multicenter cohort, three-phase reader study, and reliance calibration analysis together represent evaluation rigor sufficient for substantive peer review. No single flaw is unresolvable, but reviewers must adjudicate: (1) whether the clinical scope claim is appropriately bounded relative to isolated acyanotic lesion performance; (2) whether the consensus-exclusion reference standard inflates the primary metrics in a way that materially affects the conclusions; and (3) whether prevalence-adjusted calibration analysis is feasible and should be required as a condition of acceptance.

5. Suggested Reviewer Expertise
Reviewers should cover: spatiotemporal video transformer architectures and attention-based interpretability for medical video classification; multi-view fusion and incomplete-data robustness in echocardiographic deep learning; randomized crossover human-AI interaction study design with specific attention to reliance calibration, NRI/IDI methodology, and crossover confound analysis; pediatric echocardiography and CHD diagnosis in tiered or resource-limited healthcare settings with experience spanning acyanotic and complex congenital phenotypes; and clinical AI evaluation methodology with emphasis on probabilistic calibration, prevalence adjustment, and real-world deployment readiness per DECIDE-AI and TRIPOD+AI standards.

6. State-of-the-Art Literature Review (Past 3 Years)
The echocardiography AI field has advanced substantially toward foundation-scale and collaborative frameworks. Christensen et al. (Nat. Med. 2024) developed EchoCLIP, a vision-language foundation model trained on echocardiogram-report pairs across a broad range of cardiac conditions. Vukadinovic et al. (Nature 2025/2026) subsequently published EchoPrime, a multi-video view-informed model trained on 12 million video-report pairs delivering comprehensive study-level predictions. Both represent the frontier of general echocardiographic representation learning against which CHD-CARE's ResNet-50-plus-transformer architecture should be benchmarked, but the manuscript does not engage with either. In the pediatric CHD domain, Cheng et al. (Research 2024) developed a multi-view multi-modal deep learning framework integrating 2D and Doppler TTE from Beijing Children's Hospital for VSD/ASD detection, achieving mean AUC of 0.996 internally — a direct methodological comparator that the manuscript cites but does not benchmark against. Lukyanenko et al. (Circulation 2026) developed EchoFocus-CHD, building on PanEcho's view-agnostic architecture with a transformer encoder to detect 12 critical and 8 non-critical CHD lesions, validated externally across echos from 58 countries across 6 continents — a scope that directly challenges CHD-CARE's generalizability framing. Day et al. (Ultrasound Obstet Gynecol 2024) directly studied clinician-AI collaboration for fetal atrioventricular septal defect detection and found that providing grad-CAM overlays did not improve and in some conditions worsened collaborative performance when the AI was incorrect — a finding directly contradicting CHD-CARE's visual-guidance benefit claims that demands engagement. The PROTEUS randomized controlled trial (ESC 2024) enrolled 2,341 patients across 20 UK hospitals and found that AI-augmented stress echocardiography interpretation showed no statistically significant improvement in the primary endpoint overall, but suggested potential benefit specifically for less-experienced clinicians — a real-world negative result that contextualizes CHD-CARE's controlled reader-study gains. The manuscript does not engage with either the Day et al. or PROTEUS findings, both of which represent the most directly relevant human-AI collaboration evidence and raise the question of whether visual guidance benefits generalize beyond controlled reader studies. Summary of Reviewer Concerns
Reviewer 1 (Lun Hu) — Most Critical
Four core objections: (1) methodology is incremental — combining transformers, GNNs, and attention mechanisms is well-trodden ground with no clear theoretical novelty; (2) evaluation lacks rigor — missing stronger/more recent baselines, no downstream task analysis, inadequate metrics (no F1/AUC discussion), and data leakage risk; (3) interpretability is superficial — subnetworks appear post-hoc rather than mechanistically explanatory, and the web interface demo (osteogenesis imperfecta + bosentan → 2-node subnetwork) actively undermines the interpretability claim; (4) figure quality is poor.
Reviewer 2 (Jingwen Yan) — Moderate, Mixed
Positive on the unified multi-task framing and zero-shot capability. Three pointed concerns: (1) rare disease claims are not validated with stratified analysis separating rare from common entities; (2) fixed walk length of 5 with softmax normalization may dilute prediction reliability for complex biological cases; (3) binary edge representation and lack of evidence scores are known KG limitations that are directly relevant here and inadequately discussed.
Reviewer 3 (Sir Munir Pirmohamed) — Technically Sharp
The most mechanistically sophisticated critique. Key points: random walks introduce structural bias toward topologically central rather than biologically important nodes; treating indications and contraindications as equivalent masked tokens in the same vocabulary is a pharmacological oversimplification; the MLP link prediction head suggests the encoder is not doing the heavy lifting — the context window is ignored; 5.5% AUC gain over TxGNN requires statistical significance testing, not just standard deviation reporting; the model optimizes for co-occurrence, not causality, and this is never adequately acknowledged.
Handling Editor (Eric Wang)
Decision is effectively "reject pending major revision with uncertainty." He flags partial information leakage in zero-shot settings as unresolved, and notes mechanistic subnetwork validation is qualitative only. The overall rating on the system is Reject.

Decision Recommendation
Reject, not major revision. Here is the reasoning:
The three reviewers collectively identify problems that cannot be addressed through revision alone without substantially rebuilding the paper. Specifically: the interpretability framework is structurally weak (not just underreported), the statistical evaluation design has leakage and baseline selection problems that require re-running experiments, and the pharmacological conflation of indication/contraindication as equivalent token types is a conceptual flaw, not a presentation gap.
The counterargument for revision: Reviewer 2 is broadly positive, the unified multi-task framing is genuinely novel relative to prior KG work, and the ClinicalTrials.gov external validation is a real strength that few comparable papers include. A revision pathway is not unreasonable if the authors can address the statistical significance gaps and the interpretability overclaiming.
The question you should also be asking but aren't: What is the editor's actual threshold here? Eric Wang's circulation note says "acceptance will depend on whether reviewer is convincing by generalization beyond KG structure, robustness of mechanistic interpretation." That framing suggests he sees a revision path, but Reviewer 1's code availability critique (no pinned environment, no seed/determinism settings, leakage risk in split logic) means reproducibility is also in question — which at Nature Communications is effectively a separate rejection trigger regardless of scientific merit.
Bottom line: Reject with invitation to resubmit only if the authors are willing to re-run experiments with corrected baselines, statistical significance tests, and a fundamentally revised interpretability section. A standard major revision would be insufficient.

Summary of Reviewer Concerns
Reviewer 1 (Gwydion Williams) — Thorough, Mixed-to-Critical
Five major concerns: (1) the claimed efficiency advantage is never actually measured — there is no analysis of how many tests DxEvolve requests per case, whether this number decreases with experience, or whether confident diagnoses are reached with fewer interventions over time; this is the central practical claim and it is unvalidated; (2) no frontier model baselines (Claude, GPT-4o, Gemini) are tested, which is a critical omission since the gains may reflect the DCR framework rather than DxEvolve specifically — or conversely, that frontier models already perform comparably without it; (3) the early/late DCP discussion obscures a two-regime picture: early DCPs drive most gains, late DCPs are marginal refinements, yet the paper frames them as uniformly progressive; (4) using the DCP repository to standardize the Chinese PLA cohort translations introduces circularity — the same corpus shaping the test data is the one being tested; (5) the cross-disease generalizability claim rests primarily on UTI and liver abscess, both of which are abdominal and the sample sizes are very small (UTI n=11, appendicitis n=30).
Nine minor comments cover figure clarity, ground truth labeling, interpretability of the "incorrect experience rate" metric, and the framing of clinician comparison as "surpassing" rather than "comparable."
Reviewer 3 (Antonis Armoundas) — Most Comprehensive and Damaging
Ten major concerns, organized around three core problems:
The "self-evolution" framing is misleading. The DCP construction explicitly uses ground-truth diagnosis labels, correctness flags, rule-based feedback, and clinician orders — this is supervised, label-aware case-memory consolidation, not autonomous clinical learning. The authors need to either reframe the mechanism or run ablations distinguishing raw case retrieval from DCP retrieval, label-removed DCPs, test-ordering-only guidance, and nearest-neighbor retrieval.
Several workflow-alignment results are partly prompt-enforced. The agent is instructed in its system prompt to perform physical examination, labs, imaging, experience search, and guideline search before diagnosis — this directly inflates workflow-consistency and guideline-adherence metrics. An ablation without these prompt constraints is necessary.
The external validation is too small and insufficiently reported. N=293 overall, with UTI (n=11) being the basis for several generalizability claims. The paper requires exact case counts, confidence intervals per disease and language stratum, and sensitivity analyses excluding the smallest samples. The claim of 17.1% gain on "uncovered categories" is based on only two disease categories.
Additional concerns: possible evidence leakage in the cholecystitis example trace (future imaging findings appear in the experience search before being acquired); the human comparison should not be framed as "surpassing"; governance claims are conceptual and undemonstrated; statistical reporting uses p-values without uncertainty quantification across backbone models or seeds.
Handling Editor (Eric Wang)
Rates this as borderline OTR/Reject. The framework is conceptually fresh and the process-level analysis adds substance, but the dataset is small (under 3K encounters), disease scope is narrow (~5 abdominal conditions), external validation is from a single institution in Beijing, and several central claims — governance, expert-level performance, cross-disease generalizability — exceed what the evidence supports. Decision Recommendation
Major Revision, not Reject — but with a tight scope.
The case for revision: Both reviewers end with constructive rather than dismissive language. Reviewer 3 explicitly recommends major revision. The handling editor was persuaded to proceed OTR. The conceptual contribution — iterative evidence acquisition with a non-parametric experiential repository — is genuinely differentiated from the comparable papers cited. The ablation study structure is already partially present, meaning the authors have the experimental infrastructure to address the key gaps.
The case against (strongest counterargument): The circularity problem in the PLA cohort validation is not a presentation issue — if the DCP repository was used to shape the test data translations, the external validation result may be fundamentally unreliable. This cannot be fixed without re-running the experiment with an independent translation source. Combined with the evidence leakage concern in the example trace and the prompt-enforced workflow metrics, there are three independent validity threats that each independently could require re-evaluation of core results.
The question you should also be asking: Is the self-evolution framing salvageable, or does reframing it as supervised case-memory consolidation materially change the paper's contribution claim? If the answer is yes — that the novelty depends on the autonomous learning framing — then the revision path collapses, because Reviewer 3's critique is definitional, not empirical. The authors need to answer this explicitly before revision begins.
Bottom line: Invite major revision contingent on: (1) independent translation pipeline for the PLA cohort or acknowledgment of the circularity with bounded claims; (2) ablations distinguishing DCP retrieval from raw case retrieval and prompt-enforced versus free workflow; (3) reframing of self-evolution claims with honest characterization of the supervision signal; (4) statistical significance testing with confidence intervals per stratum. If (1) requires re-running external validation from scratch and the authors cannot do this, the paper should be rejected.
